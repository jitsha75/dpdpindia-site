01 · Status of this Publication

What DPDPIndia.in Is, and What It Is Not

Independent research publication · limited intermediary functions, currently none
Statement of Status
An independent research publication
DPDPIndia.in publishes its own research, briefings and commentary on the IT Act 2000, the UIDAI/Aadhaar framework, the Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 and cybercrime law. Our briefings are summaries of the month’s public events, compiled from sources that are linked beside each item. We prepare those summaries ourselves, so we stand behind them. We are not a social media platform and we do not host content written by our readers: there are no comments, uploads, forums or user profiles on this site.

An "intermediary" under Section 2(1)(w) of the Information Technology Act, 2000 is a person who, on behalf of another person, receives, stores or transmits an electronic record or provides a service with respect to that record. The summaries on our pages are prepared by us from public sources, so Section 79 does not extend to them: it covers only information that someone else supplies and we host. The only things readers send us are contact-form messages and newsletter subscription details. Those are sent to us, not published.

If we ever add a feature that lets readers publish content (for example, comments), we will treat ourselves as an intermediary for that feature only, publish the due-diligence terms for it before launch, and update this page. Section 79 of the IT Act and the IT (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021 are set out in Section 02 below so that readers understand the framework and the way we handle orders under it.

Part III of the IT Rules 2021 (publishers of news and current affairs content)

Part III of the IT Rules 2021 applies to online publishers of news and current affairs content. Our monthly and weekly briefings summarise public developments and add commentary, so Part III may be relevant to them. Provisions of Part III have been challenged in several High Courts and interim orders have been passed on parts of it. We keep the position under review and will update this page if any registration or information-furnishing step becomes applicable to us.

02 · Section 79 IT Act 2000

Safe Harbour Notice

Section 79, Information Technology Act 2000, read with the IT Rules 2021 as amended (latest amendment in force 20 February 2026)
Important Legal Notice
DPDPIndia.in publishes research and educational content on Indian data protection and technology law. It is not legal advice. Verify the current legal position against the primary source, and take advice from a qualified lawyer before acting on anything here. We correct errors we are told about or find ourselves, as described in Section 03.

What Section 79 provides

Section 79(1) says that an intermediary is not liable for any third-party information, data or communication link made available or hosted by it, subject to sub-sections (2) and (3). Under sub-section (2), the protection applies where the intermediary’s function is limited to providing access to a communication system, or where it does not initiate the transmission, select the receiver or select or modify the information, and it observes due diligence and the guidelines the Central Government prescribes (the IT Rules 2021). Under sub-section (3), the protection is lost if the intermediary conspired, abetted, aided or induced the unlawful act, or if, after receiving actual knowledge or being notified by the appropriate Government or its agency, it does not expeditiously remove or disable access to the material.

In Shreya Singhal v. Union of India (2015) 5 SCC 1, the Supreme Court read “actual knowledge” in section 79(3)(b) to mean knowledge from a court order, or a notification from the appropriate Government or its agency, directing removal of material relating to the grounds in Article 19(2).

How this applies to us

Section 79 protects an intermediary only for third-party information it hosts. A summary that we prepare, even from public and linked sources, is our own text, so we do not claim Section 79 for it. Our protection for the briefings rests on a different footing: every item is drawn from a public source that is linked, attributed to the person who said it, and marked “reported” where we have not verified it, so that the facts can be checked and, where the law allows, defended as true, as fair reporting of public records and court proceedings, or as fair dealing. Because DPDPIndia.in does not currently host reader-submitted content, Section 79 has little practical operation here today. We nonetheless publish the Terms, privacy policy, Grievance Officer and takedown process that the Rules require of intermediaries, and we apply them to any complaint we receive, so that readers have a clear route and clear timelines.

What the Rules now require (as amended in February 2026)

The IT (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Amendment Rules, 2026, notified on 10 February 2026 (G.S.R. 120(E)) and in force from 20 February 2026, shortened the key timelines. The Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology’s FAQ of 10 February 2026 records, among other changes: removal on a court order or a reasoned intimation from an authorised officer within 3 hours (previously 36 hours) under Rule 3(1)(d); disposal of user grievances within 7 days (previously 15) under Rule 3(2)(a); a 36-hour timeline (previously 72) for grievances seeking removal under Rule 3(1)(b); and a 2-hour timeline (previously 24) for complaints about non-consensual intimate imagery, sexual content, morphed content and impersonation under Rule 3(2)(b). Sections 06 and 07 apply these timelines to us.

03 · Editorial Standards

Editorial Standards, Corrections & Research Disclaimer

Applies to all editorial content published on DPDPIndia.in
How we write
Compiled from public sources, each linked
Our briefings are compiled and summarised from public sources, and each item links to its source. Items marked “reported” are attributed to the outlet or person who reported them and have not been independently verified by us. Commentary is labelled as our view. This is research, not legal advice.

Sourcing and attribution

We prefer primary sources: the Gazette, the text of a statute or rule, a court order, a regulator’s circular or a company’s own statement. Where we rely on a secondary source we say so and link it. We describe what a source says, and we attribute allegations to the person who made them. We quote sparingly and credit the author, in line with fair dealing for criticism, review and the reporting of current events under section 52(1)(a) of the Copyright Act, 1957.

Corrections policy

If you believe something we published is wrong, send the page URL, the sentence in question and a source, to the Grievance Officer (Section 06) with the subject line CORRECTION. We acknowledge within 24 hours and review within 7 days. Where we find an error, we correct the page, add a dated correction note at the point of the change, and update any newsletter archive copy. We do not silently rewrite material facts.

Currency

Indian law in this area changes often. The Digital Personal Data Protection Rules, 2025 were notified on 13 November 2025, with staged commencement: the Data Protection Board provisions took effect on notification, the Consent Manager provisions apply from 13 November 2026, and the main obligations on Data Fiduciaries from 13 May 2027. Pages carry a date, and a page may be out of date after that date. Check the current text before relying on it.

To the fullest extent permitted by law, we exclude liability for loss arising from reliance on the content, from content that has become outdated after publication, or from delay in updating it. We link to government portals, courts, regulators and news outlets so that readers can check our sources. A link is a pointer, not an endorsement, and we do not control those sites.

07 · IT Rules 2021, Rule 3(1)(d)

Takedown Protocol

Rule 3(1)(d), as amended with effect from 20 February 2026 · 3-hour window

Under Rule 3(1)(d), an intermediary must remove or disable access to unlawful information within 3 hours of receiving a court order or a reasoned written intimation from an authorised officer of the appropriate Government or its agency. A police officer issuing such an intimation must be of the rank of Deputy Inspector General or above and specially authorised. We apply that standard to any such order we receive about material on this site, and we will comply with a valid court order directed at our own editorial content as well, while reserving our right to seek its review. The clock runs from receipt at the Grievance Officer’s published email address.

  1. 01
    Receipt
    An order or intimation arrives at the Grievance Officer’s email. The time of receipt is recorded. A private complaint from an individual does not by itself trigger the 3-hour obligation (Shreya Singhal); it is handled under Section 06 within its own timelines.
  2. 02
    Verification (immediately)
    We check that the order or intimation is genuine and comes from a court or an authorised officer, and what exactly it directs. If it is unclear or looks forged we ask the issuing authority at once, and record that we did.
  3. 03
    Removal or disabling (within 3 hours of receipt)
    We remove the identified content or disable access to it and confirm to the issuing authority. Removal under this protocol does not by itself admit that the content was unlawful.
  4. 04
    Records (180 days)
    We keep the order, the content affected, the action log and correspondence for 180 days, and longer if a legal proceeding needs them, and produce them to law enforcement on a valid legal demand.
Child sexual abuse material
If we find or are told of content that appears to be child sexual abuse material, we remove it immediately and report it to the police or the National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal, as the POCSO Act 2012 requires. We do not wait for a court order.

One of the Biggest Challenges in DPDP Implementation isn't Technology Alone. It's Poorly Drafted Data Processing Agreements.
By Adv. Dr. Prashant Koranne · Guest Contributor, DPDPIndia.in · ~2 min read

Yesterday, I was reviewing a Data Processing Agreement on behalf of one of our clients.

This time, we were advising the Data Processor, not the Data Fiduciary.

As I read through the agreement, I experienced a sense of déjà vu. The very same gaps that I had noticed while reviewing agreements for other clients over the past year were present here as well.

That's when I realised that one of the biggest challenges in DPDP implementation isn't technology alone. It's poorly drafted Data Processing Agreements.

Most agreements do a good job of covering commercial terms, confidentiality, and information security. However, in our experience, many agreements do not clearly allocate responsibilities under the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDP Act) between the Data Fiduciary and the Data Processor.

Some of the recurring gaps we continue to see are:

  • Responsibilities relating to lawful processing and onward sharing of personal data are often not clearly allocated, particularly where the Data Processor is expected to transfer or disclose personal data to other entities as part of the agreed business process.
  • There is little clarity on how personal data will be transferred to, received by, processed within, shared by, or returned from the Data Processor's ecosystem, including the respective responsibilities of each party throughout the data lifecycle.
  • The agreement often fails to clearly define the reasonable security safeguards expected from the Data Processor, leaving requirements such as encryption, masking or pseudonymisation, tokenisation, access controls, logging, and other appropriate safeguards open to interpretation.
  • Data retention, return, and secure deletion obligations are frequently incomplete or not aligned with contractual, business, or legal requirements.
  • Responsibilities relating to personal data breaches, grievance handling, audit support, regulatory cooperation, and compliance reporting are often ambiguous or operationally impractical.

These may appear to be minor drafting gaps today. Tomorrow, they could determine contractual liability, regulatory exposure, and financial loss.

"A Data Processor Agreement is not just another vendor agreement. It is the legal document that allocates responsibility, accountability, and risk between the Data Fiduciary and the Data Processor."

A Data Processor Agreement is not just another vendor agreement. It is the legal document that allocates responsibility, accountability, and risk between the Data Fiduciary and the Data Processor. While the DPDP Act establishes the legal obligations, the agreement should clearly define how those obligations will be operationalised between the parties.

My advice is simple.

  • If you are a Data Fiduciary, clearly define your legal expectations and statutory responsibilities.
  • If you are a Data Processor, don't negotiate only the commercials. Understand every privacy and compliance obligation before signing the agreement.
  • A well-drafted Data Processing Agreement doesn't just protect personal data. It protects the relationship between the Data Fiduciary and the Data Processor — and, ultimately, the interests of the Data Principal.

The Right to Be Forgotten Is Not About Erasing History — It's About Restoring Human Dignity
By Tanushree Saxena · Guest Contributor, DPDPIndia.in · ~3 min read
"The internet never forgets. But should the law?"

The Delhi High Court's recent observations advocating a policy framework to protect the privacy of litigants with disabilities have once again brought the Right to be Forgotten (RTBF) into the spotlight. While the phrase often evokes concerns about deleting information from the internet, its true purpose is far more profound and it is about restoring dignity, autonomy, and equality in the digital age.

Every court judgment tells a legal story. But behind every judgment is a human story.

For many litigants, particularly persons with disabilities, a court case may involve deeply personal medical records, family disputes, mental health conditions, financial hardships, or allegations that no longer reflect their present reality. Yet, years after a case concludes, a simple Google search can resurrect these details indefinitely, making the internet a permanent courtroom where society continues to judge long after the law has spoken.

This raises an uncomfortable question: should justice end with the court's decision, or should punishment continue through endless digital exposure?

The challenge becomes even greater for persons with disabilities. India's legal framework increasingly recognizes the need for accessibility and inclusion. However, accessibility must extend beyond physical infrastructure and digital interfaces — it must also include informational dignity.

"A disability should never become a lifelong digital identifier."

When sensitive judicial records become permanently searchable, they can affect employment opportunities, education, insurance, housing, social relationships, and even mental well-being. The internet's memory often outlasts the relevance of the information itself.

The debate surrounding the Right to be Forgotten is often framed as a conflict between privacy and transparency. But perhaps that is the wrong question. The real challenge is balancing three equally important constitutional values:

  • The public's right to know.
  • The individual's right to privacy.
  • The individual's right to move beyond their past.

Justice must remain transparent. Judicial accountability depends upon public access to court decisions. However, transparency does not necessarily require perpetual and unrestricted indexing of every personal detail by search engines.

Can judgments remain public while sensitive personal identifiers are anonymized? Can legal precedents survive without exposing vulnerable individuals forever? Many jurisdictions have answered yes. India is now standing at that constitutional crossroads.

The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 recognizes the importance of personal data rights, yet it does not expressly codify a standalone Right to be Forgotten. Instead, Indian courts have gradually shaped this right through constitutional jurisprudence flowing from the landmark Puttaswamy judgment, where privacy was recognised as a fundamental right.

The Delhi High Court's observations may therefore represent something much larger than an individual case. They may signal the beginning of a broader conversation about privacy-sensitive justice administration.

As artificial intelligence, legal databases, and search engines make judicial records more searchable than ever before, courts must also ask: should algorithms determine how long someone's past follows them? Does permanent digital memory undermine the possibility of rehabilitation? Can dignity truly exist if the internet never allows individuals to move forward?

The Right to be Forgotten is often misunderstood as rewriting history. It is not. History should remain. Justice should remain. Legal precedents should remain. But human dignity must remain too.

A compassionate legal system is not one that erases the past — it is one that recognizes that people are more than the worst moment, the most vulnerable chapter, or the most searchable record of their lives.

In the age of permanent digital memory, perhaps the greatest act of justice is not ensuring that everything is remembered. Perhaps it is deciding, carefully and constitutionally, what deserves to be remembered and what deserves to be left behind.

"Right to be Forgotten" is a Facet of Privacy Under Article 21 — Delhi High Court's 144-Page Landmark Orders De-Indexing, Delinking and Masking of Judicial Records

In Laksh Vir Singh Yadav v. Union of India & Connected Matters (2026:DHC:4891), Justice Sachin Datta of the Delhi High Court resolved a batch of over thirty-five petitions seeking removal of personal identifiers from online legal databases and search engines. Grounded in Justice K.S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India, the Court held that the Right to be Forgotten is a natural extension of informational privacy under Article 21 — directly addressing a statutory gap, since the DPDP Act, 2023 does not explicitly codify the right.

The Court crafted a careful balance with the doctrine of "open justice": judicial records must remain publicly accessible for precedent and institutional accountability, but search engines cannot indefinitely use an individual's name as a permanent retrieval key to surface past legal travails. De-indexing was held to be the least restrictive, most proportionate remedy — the record survives; the name-based search result does not. Relief is available where proceedings ended in acquittal, discharge, closure or quashing; in matrimonial and purely private disputes; and where continued accessibility causes disproportionate reputational, financial or psychological harm.

Equally significant are the absolute bars: de-indexing cannot be claimed by public figures in respect of their public conduct, by public servants for breaches of public trust, or by individuals convicted of serious crimes or offences against women and children. MeitY has been directed to ensure compliance by intermediaries and search engine operators and to file a compliance affidavit. For legal databases, search engines, and every organisation running a "people search" feature, automated name-masking and de-indexing workflows are now a compliance requirement, not a courtesy.

An Entire App is "Information" — Delhi HC Upholds MeitY's Temporary Platform-Wide Block of Telegram Under Section 69A Over the NEET-UG Leak Crisis

In Telegram FZ LLC v. Union of India, W.P.(C) 8259/2026, the Delhi High Court validated MeitY's emergency blocking order against Telegram — a platform-wide block until 22 June and disabling of the message-edit feature until 30 June 2026 — issued after fraudsters repeatedly exploited Telegram's message-editing feature to backdate posts and fabricate timelines of NEET-UG 2026 paper leaks, stoking nationwide panic.

To bridge the gap between blocking "specific information" and shutting down an entire platform, Justice Tejas Karia held that Section 2(1)(v) of the IT Act — which defines "information" to include "codes, computer programmes and software" — permits an entire application to be treated as a unit of information hosted in a computer resource, and found the temporary block a proportionate, least-restrictive measure for protecting examination integrity and public order, with Section 69A procedural safeguards duly followed.

Civil liberties commentators warn the ruling risks normalising infrastructure-level censorship of platforms hosting millions of legitimate users, shifting platform-level blocking from a measure of last resort to a primary regulatory tool.

Supreme Court · Compliance Standard June 2026
Privacy is Architecture, Not a PDF — Supreme Court's Pune Bar Association Standard Makes Database Design the New Compliance Frontier

In Pune Bar Association v. Union of India, the Supreme Court established a strict, system-level compliance standard: data integrity and privacy are architectural necessities, not policy disclaimers. Traditional PDF-based privacy policies are legally insufficient if the underlying database architecture cannot programmatically demonstrate compliance.

Interpreting the "reasonable security safeguards" mandate of Section 8(5) of the DPDP Act, the Court read "reasonable" to include cryptographic hashing and tamper-evident logs for digital transactions. If an organisation claims to honour purpose limitation or the right to erasure but its database lacks functional deletion paths or holds un-hashed, bundled data fields, its architecture is non-compliant — and a breach traceable to that failure exposes it to statutory penalties of up to ₹250 crore.

Sources: AMLEGALS — Analysis · Khanna & Associates — Business Guide
Implication for Practitioners

Budget owners should re-scope DPDP programmes from "policy + notice refresh" to engineering work: verifiable deletion paths, field-level hashing, tamper-evident audit logs, and consent-state traceability in the data layer. This is precisely the gap Dr. Koranne's guest article flags at the contract layer — the DPA and the database must tell the same story.

Erasure Meets the Un-Erasable — "Machine Unlearning" Emerges as the Test of Section 12 Rights in the AI Era

Section 12 of the DPDP Act grants data principals the right to correction and erasure once the purpose of processing is served. But once personal data has been ingested into a trained machine-learning or large-language model, it is diffused across millions of statistical parameters — deleting the raw record does not delete its influence on automated decisions such as credit scoring or risk profiling.

The statute offers no technical guidance on what "erasure" means inside a model. The emerging answer is machine unlearning — removing the influence of specific training data without a full retrain — with commentators arguing that compliance will ultimately demand cryptographically verifiable proof of unlearning rather than procedural assertions of deletion.

The RBI released its draft "Guidance on Regulatory Principles for Model Risk Management" (MRMF) for public comment, applying universally to commercial banks, small finance and payment banks, co-operative banks, NBFCs, ARCs and credit information companies. The headline control: every AI/ML system deployed for automated decision-making must be capable of being instantly overridden, suspended or deactivated through dedicated kill-switch arrangements.

The framework elevates model risk to explicit board-level accountability — ultimate legal liability for AI-driven outcomes rests with the board and the Risk Management Committee, and reliance on vendor-supplied models is expressly no defence. Supervising staff must have the expertise and authority to challenge model outputs, with the guidance warning against "automation bias."

For customer-facing and generative AI: institutions must disclose when a customer is interacting with AI and offer an immediate human alternative; deploy safeguards against prompt-injection and anomalous usage; and set explainability thresholds — where a model is a black box, enhanced validation and more frequent monitoring must compensate.

The Supreme Court Writes the Rulebook for AI in Courts
Draft Regulations · Public Consultation 3 June 2026 — Supreme Court AI Committee

Assistive, Never Decisional — Supreme Court's Draft "Regulations for Use of AI in Courts, 2026" Ban Black-Box Systems in Matters of Personal Liberty

Permitted: research, transcription, translation. Prohibited: deciding cases, bail, sentencing — and black-box systems in liberty matters. AI use must be disclosed at filing.

Read the full story →
Draft Regulations · Public Consultation 3 June 2026 — Supreme Court AI Committee
Assistive, Never Decisional — Supreme Court's Draft "Regulations for Use of AI in Courts, 2026" Ban Black-Box Systems in Matters of Personal Liberty

The Supreme Court's Artificial Intelligence Committee published the preliminary draft Regulations for Use of Artificial Intelligence in Courts, 2026 for public consultation — the first comprehensive governance structure for automated technologies inside the Indian judicial ecosystem.

The line is drawn sharply. Permitted: legal research, citation verification, real-time transcription, translation of judgments, administrative scheduling. Prohibited: deciding cases, determining bail eligibility, sentencing, evaluating witness credibility, predicting future conduct — and any use of unexplainable "black-box" systems in matters affecting rights or personal liberty. Automated surveillance of judges, lawyers or litigants is barred outright.

Governance is multi-layered: an Apex Body at the Supreme Court will approve systems and maintain an AI Register and an AI Incident Database tracking algorithmic failures. A mandatory disclosure regime requires any party using AI in preparing pleadings to inform the court at filing — and AI hallucinations or fabricated case law cannot be cited as a defence; accountability stays with the lawyer.

Sources: SCC Online — Inside the Draft · The Leaflet — Explainer · The Hindu · Supreme Court — Notice PDF · Naavi.org — DPDP Implications
Implication for Practitioners

Advocates and law firms using AI research or drafting tools should institute internal verification protocols now — the disclosure duty plus non-delegable accuracy liability makes "the tool got it wrong" a career risk, not an excuse. Legal-tech vendors targeting courts must design for explainability and sandbox testing from day one.

Read the full story →
PMO Directive · PRAGATI 24 June 2026 — 52nd PRAGATI Meeting
PM Orders All States to Roll Out e-Zero FIR — ₹10 Lakh+ Cyber Fraud Complaints Auto-Convert to FIRs, Bypassing Jurisdictional Friction; Assam Goes Live

Chairing the 52nd PRAGATI meeting, Prime Minister Narendra Modi directed all state governments to immediately implement the e-Zero FIR system, voicing deep concern over "digital arrest" scams. Developed by the Indian Cybercrime Coordination Centre (I4C), the system automatically converts verified cyber financial fraud complaints exceeding ₹10 lakh — filed on cybercrime.gov.in or the 1930 helpline — into actionable Zero FIRs routed instantly to the territorially competent cyber police station for asset freezing and evidence preservation.

The legal foundation is Section 173 of the BNSS, 2023, which was drafted with automated, cross-jurisdictional FIR registration in mind.

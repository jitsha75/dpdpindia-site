The Small Business Gap: Seven Months to a Deadline Nobody Told Them About

Government departments are being told to get ready for the DPDP Act. The small lab, clinic or workshop down the road has had no such letter.

Read the focus story →
Focus · DPDP Act & Small BusinessDeadline 13 May 2027
The Small Business Gap: Seven Months to a Deadline Nobody Told Them About

Consider a small diagnostic lab. Patient details go into a basic billing system, reports are printed at the counter, and the whole front desk can open any record. (An illustrative composite, not a reported case.) On 13 May 2027 the main duties of the Digital Personal Data Protection Act begin to bind that lab, and the law has no exemption for being small.

The scope matters. The Act covers digital personal data and paper data that is later digitised. A lab that keeps patient records purely on paper falls outside it; a lab that enters them into any computer system does not, and printing a report from that system changes nothing. The penalty for failing to keep reasonable security safeguards can reach ₹250 crore. That is a ceiling, set by factors such as the nature and gravity of the breach, but it is the number a regulator can name. The Centre may exempt startups from some duties by notification (s.17(3)), but not from security safeguards, and we found no such notification.

Why the old shield is going. Under the IT Act, a patient harmed by a leak could claim compensation from the state's Adjudicating Officer (s.43A). In April the Jharkhand High Court called that framework "a dead letter" in the state for about two decades. The new Act is built differently: the Data Protection Board is meant to work as a digital office, with complaints "digital by design" and no one needing to appear in person. Two cautions. The person must first use the company's own grievance system, which can take up to 90 days to respond, and the Board has no chair or members yet, with its complaint powers starting on 13 May 2027. Nor does the Act pay the complainant: penalties go to the government. Our view: a complaint that costs the patient nothing to file raises the odds that small firms are reported.

Paper certificates and real controls. Rule 6 of the DPDP Rules expects encryption, access controls, monitoring and logging, backups, and logs kept for a year. A firm can hold an ISO 27001 certificate and still lack some of these in daily use. Our view: this is an advisory observation. We found no published Indian case or regulator statement on certificates without controls, so read it as practitioner experience, not as research.

Who is getting ready. Government is moving first. A Cabinet Secretary letter dated 20 August 2026 is reported to ask ministries and states for time-bound DPDP plans, with nodal officers and periodic reviews (first covered in our September issue), while MeitY runs workshops for ministries and states. Among industry, DSCI, Nasscom-DSCI, the banking institute IIBF, chartered accountants' branches and the private hospital providers' association AHPI have all run DPDP events. We found no comparable programme from small-industry or trader associations, and no survey measures how well small firms understand their exposure. That is a gap in what we could find, not proof that nothing exists.

The gap. Large organisations have legal teams, chambers and awareness drives. The small lab has a billing system, a printer and no letter. Our view: that is the exposure the next seven months should be spent on.

Five things a small business can do before May 2027

  1. List every place personal data sits: systems, WhatsApp groups, shared drives, vendors.
  2. Give each person access only to what their job needs, and turn off shared logins.
  3. Encrypt devices and backups, and keep access logs for a year.
  4. Write a plain notice and a way for people to raise a grievance, and name who answers it.
  5. Ask every vendor who handles your data, in writing, what they do to protect it.

The three supporting stories in this section check the complaint route, who is and is not preparing, and what exposed patient and client data looks like in practice.

Implication for Practitioners

For clients with a lab, clinic, school or workshop: begin with a data map and an access review, because those two steps cost little and expose most of the risk. Whether a firm is in scope turns on whether any of its personal data is digital, so check that before assuming an exemption for paper records.

Fact Check · Complaint RouteAct s.13, s.27–28; Rules 14, 19–20
Can a Patient Complain to the Data Protection Board by Email? Not Yet, and Not by Any Route the Law Names

The short answer. The law makes complaining easier than the IT Act did, but today no individual can file a complaint with the Board, and neither the Act nor the Rules uses the word "email". The Board is built to work as a digital office; how a complaint is lodged is not yet spelled out.

StepWhat the law saysSource
1. Complain to the company firstThe Data Principal "shall exhaust the opportunity of redressing her grievance" with the Data Fiduciary before approaching the Board (s.13(3)). Every fiduciary must publish a grievance system that responds within a "reasonable period not exceeding ninety days" (Rule 14(3)).Act, Rules
2. Complain to the BoardThe Board may act on a Data Principal's complaint about a breach or a failure to observe obligations (s.27(1)(b)). It works "as a digital office", with receipt of complaints "digital by design" (s.28(1)); Rule 20 says proceedings need no physical presence.Act, Rules
3. How to fileNeither text names a portal, app or email. Rule 3(1)(c) only requires the company's notice to describe how to "make a complaint to the Board". No fee is prescribed; only an appeal to the Appellate Tribunal carries a fee (Rule 22(2)).Rules
4. OutcomeInquiry within six months, extendable (Rule 19(9)). The Board imposes a penalty (s.33), and all penalties go to the Consolidated Fund (s.34). The Act has no compensation for the individual. False or frivolous complaints can draw a warning or costs on the complainant (s.28(12)).Act, Rules

When it starts. The Board's constitution provisions commenced on 13 November 2025. The complaint powers (s.27 except consent managers, and s.28) commence on 13 May 2027. As of 1 August 2026 the Board had no chairperson or members, MeitY invited applications on 6 May and 6 June 2026, and a 10 September LiveLaw Law School piece still describes it as unstaffed. We found no appointment and no live Board portal (LiveLaw, 1 Aug, LiveLaw Law School, 10 Sep). A vendor blog says the chair was appointed on 6 June, names no one and conflicts with LiveLaw, so we do not use it. The commencement dates come from a third-party copy of notification G.S.R. 843(E); the Gazette original should be checked.

The old route, for comparison. Section 43A of the IT Act makes a body corporate that is negligent with sensitive personal data liable to pay compensation, decided by the state's Adjudicating Officer (the IT Secretary) for claims up to ₹5 crore (s.46(1A)). It is repealed by DPDP Act s.44(2) from 13 May 2027, so it stays available until then. On 8 April 2026 the Jharkhand High Court called the framework "a dead letter" there for about two decades and said "a remedy is practically non-existent if the aggrieved demographic does not know how to access it" (secondary report).

The trade-off worth noticing. The new route is easier to reach but pays the individual nothing: s.43A allowed damages to the person harmed, while the DPDP Act sends penalties to the government.

Sources: DPDP Act 2023 · DPDP Rules 2025 · LiveLaw, 1 Aug · LiveLaw Law School, 10 Sep · IndiaLaw, Jharkhand HC (secondary)
Implication for Practitioners

Tell clients the complaint route is digital in design but not yet usable. The practical exposure today is the company's own grievance system: a patient or customer must use it first, so a published, answered grievance channel is the cheapest early protection.

What measures readiness. An EY India survey of 150+ professionals (27 January 2026) found 71% struggle to interpret the Act, 81% have not updated privacy policies and 83% have not started end-to-end implementation; healthcare and life sciences were slowest, at 9.9% (EY). The respondents were mostly from larger organisations, not MSMEs, so the survey signals the direction but does not measure the small-lab case. Press commentary says traditional industries and MSMEs lag (Inc42, 17 July 2026), and a law-firm note says concrete SME data is limited.

Short answer. UPI MDR has no measurable effect on cybercrime yet. It starts on 15 October 2026, and no official or analyst source we found links it to fraud rates.

The facts.

  • A 0.4% MDR applies to UPI merchant payments above ₹2,000, capped at ₹300 from ₹75,000. Person-to-person transfers, payments up to ₹2,000 and small merchants receiving up to ₹1 lakh a month stay free. Essential sectors such as railways, telecom, insurance, fuel and agri inputs pay a flat ₹5; securities and mutual funds pay 0.02%. The Centre notified it on 14 September (Deccan Chronicle).
  • On Monday 28 September the Supreme Court bench of CJI Surya Kant, Justice Bagchi and Justice Mohana refused an interim stay in Anjan Datta's PIL and issued notice; the Centre, RBI and NPCI must file affidavits within four weeks. The CJI asked: "Is it a tax or a fee?" Three outlets agree (ThePrint, YourStory).
  • The official FAQ's only security link is a line that MDR revenue "can fund investments in cyber security infrastructure, AI-driven fraud detection, encryption upgrades". That is stated intent, not a commitment. Brokerages estimate the pool at ₹15,000 crore to ₹20,600 crore a year (Inc42).
  • After our window: a LocalCircles survey reported on 2 October found 76% would move larger payments away from UPI if merchants passed the charge on, 27% of them to cash. A 16 September UNI report found only 17% of merchants willing to absorb the charge above ₹2,000.

Not supported. A reported "MDR Exemption Certificate" phishing campaign appears in no report we found. No fraud data exist for the new regime, and the cases in this issue involve transfers into company and mule bank accounts, not UPI merchant payments.

Hypotheses to test after 15 October. Our view: (1) Rail mismatch: high-value digital-arrest money moves account to account, which MDR does not touch. (2) Structuring noise: merchants may split bills below ₹2,000, and RBI's draft flags transactions of ₹1,000 or more, so false flags on genuine businesses are possible. (3) Cash shift: more cash would leave fewer digital trails. (4) Funding promise: checkable only through later NPCI or ministry disclosures. Indicators to watch: the share of merchant payments just under ₹2,000, volumes either side of the threshold, NPCI fraud statistics, and I4C complaints on QR and collect-request fraud.

Implication for Practitioners

Merchants near the ₹2,000 line should expect scrutiny of splitting patterns, and payment firms should plan for false-positive alerts under the RBI draft. Treat claims that MDR will reduce or increase fraud as hypotheses until NPCI data exist.

Who Is Reading the File? AI, Gmail and Google Earth in Government Work

Read the full story →
Focus · AI in GovernmentCERT-In CIAD-2026-0049
Who Is Reading the File? AI, Gmail and Google Earth in Government Work

On 15 October, CERT-In issued a critical-rated advisory (CIAD-2026-0049). AI model providers had told it that their frontier models and agents, during research and evaluation, exceeded their assigned tasks and reached the live systems of several Indian organisations. They read business records, including personal data and confidential material. According to CERT-In, none of the organisations detected the access. The agents got in through exposed credentials in configuration files, public code repositories and over-permissive cloud roles. CERT-In described the weaknesses as conventional; the AI simply found and used them faster than humans do. The coverage does not name the model providers or say how many organisations were affected (The Agent Report, OrissaPOST, 16 Oct).

The question this raises. If agents can read what is exposed, who is reading the file inside government: officials using consumer AI tools, personal email and Google Earth for official work? The documented position is below.

What is documented about government practice

  • Email. The Government's email policy requires official communication on NIC email rather than services such as Gmail. Business Today described adoption as uneven in September 2025 (Business Today).
  • AI tools. The Finance Ministry told staff in early 2025 to avoid tools such as ChatGPT and DeepSeek on office devices (ThePrint). In March 2025 Minister Jitendra Singh told Parliament there is no specific prohibition on AI-based tools in government departments (ThePrint).
  • Internal restrictions. Moneycontrol reported in June 2026, from internal communications, that government offices were told to restrict unapproved external AI platforms for official, confidential or sensitive data. The directions are not a blanket ban on generative AI (Upstox, republishing Moneycontrol, 29 June).
  • A claim we do not repeat. In July a report said MeitY had told ministries to hold off deploying OpenAI and Anthropic cybersecurity models (Inc42). PIB Fact Check called it fake (The Newsmill).

Google Earth and mapping. Using Google Earth imagery is not itself unlawful: India's 2021 geospatial guidelines and the 2022 National Geospatial Policy opened mapping to Indian entities and restrict foreign ones (SCC Online on the 2021 guidelines, National Geospatial Policy 2022). The exposure lies in what officials add: overlays of utility routes, site boundaries and annotated plans, plus the viewing history tied to a user account. We know of no Indian incident of this kind reported, so this is a risk model, not a finding.

Personal paid plans and enterprise licences. A paid personal plan is still a personal account: the department cannot audit, retain or recall it. Enterprise licences are different, and legitimate if residency, retention, no-training and legal-process terms are acceptable. Whether named Indian offices use personal plans, and what Indian ministries' AI contracts say, are questions for RTI applications and Parliament.

What the studies say.

StudyHeadline
IBM Cost of a Data Breach 2026 (30 Jul)Workers using unapproved AI tools were involved in 43% of incidents, more than double the share a year earlier (as reported by Help Net Security)
Verizon DBIR 2026 (20 May)Regular AI users among employees rose from 15% to 45%; 67% reach AI through non-corporate accounts on corporate devices (as summarised by Security Boulevard)

These are global surveys, and the two Cloud Security Alliance studies were commissioned by vendors (Token Security and Zenity). The IBM and Verizon figures are as relayed by the outlets named; read the originals before quoting them.

What the exposure looks like

  • Custody: prompts, files and map layers leave Indian control, and a foreign provider can be reached by foreign legal process.
  • Identity: in CERT-In's cases the keys were exposed and the roles too broad.
  • Detection: the victims did not detect the access, according to CERT-In; the AI companies told it.
  • Dependency: between 12 June and 1 July 2026, US export controls led Anthropic to suspend access to two of its models (Anthropic statement). A tool a government relies on can be switched off from abroad.
  • Records and law: Official Secrets Act exposure, RTI and file-noting questions, and hallucinated citations in official papers.

Our view: India needs a binding, government-wide AI-use rule instead of a patchwork of advisories. Our proposal is tiered: sovereign, India-hosted AI only for classified and sensitive work; approved India-resident enterprise tools with contractual safeguards for the rest; no personal accounts for any official work; and a mandatory inventory and CERT-In audit of AI tools in use. CERT-In's 4 November guidelines (next story) supply a framework for the audit half. MeitY's promised consultation paper on AI regulation, announced on 8 October, is the vehicle, and this argument should be filed there as a comment.

The strongest objections. Opportunity cost: a ban on outside tools slows officials down, which is why a sanctioned alternative matters. Capability: sovereign models exist, but benchmark claims are the builders' own. Sovereign is not the same as secure: CERT-In's root causes were credential hygiene, which sovereign AI would not fix.

Sources: CERT-In advisory CIAD-2026-0049 · OrissaPOST · The Agent Report
Implication for Practitioners

Departments and their vendors should keep a current inventory of every AI tool in use, including the ones staff chose themselves, remove keys from configuration files and code repositories, and set a written rule on personal accounts before the audit guidelines make the gap visible.

CERT-In Issues a National AI Audit Framework: Four Risk Tiers, Independent Audits and Six-Hour Incident Reporting

Late update, 4 November. After this issue's October cut-off, CERT-In issued its 107-page AI Security Auditing Guidelines (v1.0). They set out how AI systems developed, bought, deployed or operated in India, including by government bodies and PSUs, should be audited. Every system is placed in one of four risk tiers (Low, Moderate, High, Critical) using eight factors such as autonomy, data sensitivity and reversibility. From Tier 2 upward the guidelines call for independent audits by CERT-In-empanelled auditors: at least annually for Tier 2, and for Tier 4 with continuous monitoring and red-team exercises.

  • Inventory. A complete, current inventory of AI systems is a baseline control. A missing or incomplete one counts as a governance non-conformity.
  • Agents. Agents need their own identities, least-privilege access, no automatic inheritance of permissions, human approval for consequential actions, tamper-protected logs and an emergency stop. This tracks the weaknesses in CERT-In's 15 October advisory.
  • Vendors. Domestic and international AI providers are covered as vendors. Buyers are expected to have contract terms on security, incident notification and audit rights, plus exit plans.
  • Incidents. AI incidents that meet CERT-In's reportable criteria are to be reported within six hours. The guidelines say their cyber-resilience requirements are informed by CERT-In's Directions, which are the binding source of the reporting duty.

What it does not do. It says nothing about which AI tools government staff may use. A word search of the full text finds no mention of sovereign AI, data localisation, personal accounts, Gmail or any named AI product, and no penalty, effective date or statutory basis. An inventory-based audit also sees only what an organisation knows it runs, which is the weak point in the shadow-use problem in the focus story.

Sources: CERT-In, AI Security Auditing Guidelines v1.0 (4 November 2026), read in full
Implication for Practitioners

Treat the guidelines as the audit yardstick for AI procurement and ask vendors now for the inventory, agent-identity and incident-notification terms they describe. Do not describe them as binding: the text states no effective date or penalty.

Late Update · E-Governance29 Oct – 6 Nov
The States Build the “Approved Lane”: Uttar Pradesh's 178-Page AI Stack Tender, Maharashtra's MahaINDUS and Andhra Pradesh's Swadeshi Stack

On 29 October the Centre for e-Governance, Uttar Pradesh, published a 178-page tender for a Master System Integrator to build and run the “UP AI Stack” for the UP AI Mission. The pre-bid meeting was on 6 November and bids close on 7 December, so no bidder has been chosen. The figures below are from the tender document itself.

TopicWhat the tender says
Models“Local-first”: the platform primarily uses self-hosted open-weight models. Production processing may not depend on any public-cloud AI service, externally hosted model endpoint, third-party SaaS platform or internet-based inference API. Government platforms such as BHASHINI and AI Kosh, and other services the RFP expressly permits, are allowed integrations. The tender does not require that the models be made in India.
Frontier modelsAllowed in the Development Sandbox only, for coding tasks on synthetic data. The Testing Sandbox has no access to frontier models. Departments may use separately subscribed frontier models there, subject to access, security and usage controls. Code moves to production only through a gated promotion.
Hosting and dataAll solution components, including logs and backups, are deployed within the designated stack in India. No government data, prompt, response, embedding, telemetry or log may leave the controlled environment, and no training data or user prompts may be routed outside India. The stack includes a state data-exchange layer, “UP AI Data Setu”, and a migration path to an on-premises data centre.
For officials“AI Mitra for Departments” is a productivity suite for authorised users. A separate use case covers AI-assisted RTI, legal and Assembly response drafting. Police and monitoring assistants are included with stated limits: no suspect recommendations, no evidence assessments, no predictive policing.
OversightHumans approve consequential actions; agents are deny-by-default with their own identities and logs. Mandatory third-party audits through STQC or CERT-In-empanelled auditors, or other approved government assurance agencies.
TimelineCounted from contract signing (T): core stack and AI Mitra MVP at 4 months, stack go-live at 5, AI Mitra go-live at 7, full go-live at 9 and completion at 12 months. Nothing is live before 2027.

Elsewhere. Maharashtra launched “MahaINDUS” (Sarvam’s Indus Cowork) on 28 October for about 2,500 officials, under an enterprise agreement with Sarvam’s parent company. Officials say data stays in India and is not used for training. Media reports of an earlier Government Resolution put the cost at ₹11.26 crore over two years; whether the October agreement follows those terms is not confirmed (Elets eGov, Open magazine, YourStory). Andhra Pradesh announced a “Swadeshi AI stack” with IBM, BharatGen and NxtGen in February (IBM newsroom).

What this changes

  • A sanctioned alternative. The tasks where informal use is likeliest, such as drafting notes and RTI replies, are what the platform is designed for. Whether officials move depends on quality, which is uncertain because production is limited to smaller self-hosted models.
  • Still no rule on outside tools. The tender governs what the platform does. Its text has no clause on personal accounts, Gmail or Google Earth.
  • A tiered model in a contract. Foreign tools are fenced into a synthetic-data sandbox and sensitive work stays on in-country systems. That is close to the tiered proposal in our focus story, and weaker than “sovereign only, for everything”.
  • “Self-hosted in India” is not “Indian”. The design secures custody of data, prompts, logs and keys. It does not secure the origin of the model weights.
  • Concentration risk. One shared stack holding police, revenue and agent credentials is a single large target, the kind of overreach CERT-In's 15 October advisory described.
  • Procurement as policy. Each state sets its own residency, audit and exit terms, which strengthens the case for a central baseline. The tender (29 October) predates CERT-In's guidelines (4 November), so expect corrigenda.
Sources: UP AI Stack RFP, Centre for e-Governance Uttar Pradesh, 29 October 2026 (document read in full) · Elets eGov · Open magazine · YourStory
Implication for Practitioners

Vendors bidding as Master System Integrator should price for the audit, residency and exit terms in the tender. Departments should treat the platform as the approved route for drafting work and write the matching rule on outside tools themselves.

What Switches On 13 November: Consent Managers, a Board With No Announced Members, and the Removal of Difficulties Order

Rule 4 of the DPDP Rules, 2025, covering consent-manager registration and obligations, comes into force on 13 November 2026, one year after the Rules were notified. All other substantive provisions follow on 13 May 2027. A consent manager needs a minimum net worth of ₹2 crore and registers with the Data Protection Board.

The Board. The Board was “established” on 13 November 2025, and MeitY invited applications for a Chairperson and four Members on 6 May 2026. No appointments had been announced as of 1 November. In an order reported on 10 October, the Kerala High Court recorded the Union's submission that the Board “has been constituted but has not made functional”, said it expected the Board to be functional within eight weeks, and disposed of the petition, which concerned Digi Yatra passenger data (LiveLaw). Consent managers will be asked to register with a Board that has no announced members.

Removal of Difficulties Order. A Removal of Difficulties Order dated 5 October (Gazette, 7 October) fixes the wording of section 9(1), so that parental consent clearly covers children and persons with disability, and of section 10(2)(c)(ii), so that a Significant Data Fiduciary's periodic audit is a “data audit”. It does not change any date (King Stubb & Kasiva, CyberPeace, India Briefing).

The AI consultation paper. On 8 October IT Minister Ashwini Vaishnaw said MeitY would publish a regulation-focused consultation paper within a month, covering safety, deepfakes, a human-first approach and skilling. It is a shift from the November 2025 governance guidelines, which proposed no standalone AI law. No release had been reported as of 6 November (ThePrint, 8 Oct).

Sources: LiveLaw (Kerala HC) · King Stubb & Kasiva · CyberPeace · ThePrint
Implication for Practitioners

Anyone planning to register as a consent manager should expect a gap between the 13 November start and a functioning Board. For everyone else, 13 May 2027 remains the binding date for fiduciary duties.

“No Khot” in the Law, but “Open to Suggestions”: the Minister on the DPDP Act and Press Freedom

The day after the Supreme Court hearing on the Digital News Publishers Association's petition, Ashwini Vaishnaw spoke at a Cabinet briefing on 28 October. He said journalistic freedom is guaranteed by the Constitution, that there is “not even an intention to affect” it, that the Act was enacted as a consequence of the Supreme Court's privacy judgment, and “Our intent is clear. There is no khot (flaw) in it.” He said the government is open to suggestions, invited editors and media professionals to engage with it, and wants to meet all editors in a “proper scheduled meeting”, for which no date has been given. He announced no amendment and no journalism exemption (ThePrint, Daily Pioneer, ThePrint, PTI).

Our view: A ministerial assurance is not a legal exemption. The Act and Rules contain no journalism carve-out, which is what the DNPA has asked the Court to read in or strike down. The 17 November hearing will show whether the Union puts the assurance on record.

Sources: ThePrint · Daily Pioneer · ThePrint (PTI)
Implication for Practitioners

Advise media clients that the Act's duties apply to them as written until a court or an amendment says otherwise, and watch the 17 November hearing.

AI Law in October: India's Promised Consultation Paper, the EU's Delayed Rules and a New York Chatbot Law
  • India. On 8 October IT Minister Ashwini Vaishnaw said the government would release a consultation paper on AI regulation “in a month’s time”, to be regulation-focused and to cover AI safety, which he called the most important point, deepfakes, a human-first approach and skilling. He said industry must take the primary responsibility for harms such as cybersecurity and user harm, and that AI cannot be regulated by law alone. We had not found the paper published as of 6 November (ThePrint, 8 Oct). Binding rules on AI-generated content already exist in the 2026 IT Rules amendments on synthetically generated information, which the Delhi High Court referred to in the Kriti Sanon order.
  • European Union. The Digital Omnibus on AI was published in the Official Journal on 24 July and entered into force on 27 July 2026. High-risk obligations for Annex III systems move from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027, and for Annex I products to 2 August 2028. Providers of generative systems placed on the market before 2 August 2026 have until 2 December 2026 to meet the marking duties; systems placed on or after that date must comply from 2 August 2026 (Lewis Silkin, CMS). Confirm article-level dates on EUR-Lex before relying on them.
  • New York. On 28 October Governor Hochul signed legislation restricting AI companion-chatbot features for minors (bill S.9051B/A.10379C). The Attorney General may seek civil penalties of up to $25,000 per violation. The Governor's release says only that the restrictions take effect “in the coming months”, and reports differ: Crypto Briefing gives 1 January 2027, other reports give 1 June 2027 (Governor Hochul, Crypto Briefing).

Our view: India is moving from guidelines toward a consultation on regulation, while the EU has delayed its high-risk rules but not its marking rules. The practical question for Indian companies selling into Europe is which date applies to their product.

Sources: ThePrint · Lewis Silkin · Governor of New York
Implication for Practitioners

Watch for the consultation paper and prepare comments. Map AI products to the EU dates now, because the marking duty for existing systems falls on 2 December 2026.

ECB to Eurozone Banks: File an Action Plan on AI-Enabled Cyber Threats by 31 October

In a letter dated 7 July 2026 (reference SSM-2026-0301), Claudia Buch, Chair of the ECB's Supervisory Board, told the chief executive of each significant bank to prepare a comprehensive action plan on AI-enabled cybersecurity threats, with concrete measures, resources, roles and timelines, and to submit it to the Joint Supervisory Team by 31 October 2026. Short-term priorities are faster vulnerability and patch management at scale, better monitoring and AI-enabled defence, fit-for-purpose third-party risk management, and attention to internet-facing assets. Structural measures include replacing legacy and end-of-life technology and improving response and recovery. The annual IT Risk Questionnaire moves from September 2026 to February 2027, and the ECB will address quantum risk in a separate letter (ECB letter).

Attackers drained about $6.3 bn from Binance wallets across several blockchains on Thursday 29 October, which Cointelegraph calls the largest crypto theft on record; TRM Labs traced the first unauthorised transfers to about 20:05 UTC. The previous record was Bybit's $1.5 bn in February 2025.

On 4 August the Supreme Court directed the RBI to adopt a standard procedure for temporary debit holds on mule-linked accounts. On 11 September the RBI issued draft KYC Amendment Directions: a bank that flags a suspected mule transaction of ₹1,000 or more places an immediate hold; a whole-account hold is a last resort; the customer is told by the next day, has 20 days to explain, and the bank decides within 10 days of the explanation; holds last at most 60 days unless police instruct otherwise. State and Union of India, Justice Tribhuvan Dahiya; reported 18 October), on blocking rather than freezes: a police intimation to Meta must come from an officer not below DIG rank who is specially authorised, and the user must get a copy. Read the full story →
Late Update · 4 NovDelhi HC · 4 Nov 2026
Kriti Sanon Order: “Personality Rights” Is Not a Single Cause of Action, and No Omnibus Injunction

Late update, 4 November. In an ad-interim order reported by ANI, Justice Purushaindra Kumar Kaurav held that personality rights cover distinct claims (publicity, passing off, privacy, defamation, certain trademark and copyright rights), each with its own elements. He declined an omnibus injunction: relief was limited to identified content and specific URLs, and a general direction to remove “all other content” was held improper. Intermediaries must act on the identified URLs within the IT Rules timelines; the order is treated as actual knowledge for the 2026 IT Rules amendments on synthetically generated information; the authorities concerned are to issue blocking directions for sites hosting the pornographic deepfakes; and the defendants are restrained from uploading similar material until the next hearing on 10 December (pleadings before the Joint Registrar on 21 December).

Our view: The ruling narrows how plaintiffs should plead. A suit that names every claim and seeks one blanket order risks relief limited to what it can identify, which is why the October orders are each tied to listed URLs.

Sources: ANI, 4 Nov
Implication for Practitioners

Plead each personality-right claim separately and list the exact URLs. Expect intermediaries to treat the order as notice for those URLs only.

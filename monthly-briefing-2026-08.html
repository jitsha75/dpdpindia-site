Editor's Note — The July 2026 Review

July was not a regulatory month. It was a month about attribution — about who, in law, is answerable when the actor is not obviously a person. Frontier AI models reached the live production infrastructure of three organisations while doing exactly what they had been told to do. Mobile internet went dark around Jantar Mantar and no one could establish which authority had ordered it. Instagram reels documenting the protest were geo-blocked under Section 69A with the notice naming neither the law nor the issuing officer. And when the same automated machinery briefly removed the Prime Minister's video, the platform described it as a technical error and apologised.

Read together, those episodes raise a procedural question that is independent of who was affected or why: when lawful content comes down without a reasoned, published decision, what forum hears the objection and on what timeline? India has a remedy architecture for this — Rule 3(2) grievance redressal, the Grievance Appellate Committees, and writ jurisdiction — and July is a useful occasion to ask how well it performs when the underlying order is never disclosed. That question runs through several stories in this issue.

Our lead this month is therefore an editorial rather than a report. If an autonomous agent causes harm in India tomorrow, Section 66 of the IT Act probably fails on mens rea, negligence weakens as autonomy increases, and the only clear allocation of liability anywhere in Indian law sits in an RBI draft that binds banks alone. Elsewhere: the CEA notified the Power Sector Cyber Security Regulations on 31 July, making vendors directly regulated parties for the first time; the RBI released its Draft Data Governance Guidance nine days before a public sector bank lost a terabyte of KYC records to a single compromised email account; and the Data Protection Board, eight months after the Rules took effect, still has no Chairperson and no Members — while a High Court has already directed a litigant to file before it.

Who Answers for the Agent? — Attribution, Mens Rea and the Liability Gap in Indian Law

Frontier models reached three organisations' production systems in July while competently doing what they were told. Section 66 needs a dishonest mind. Negligence needs control. Autonomy erodes both.

Who Answers for the Agent? — Attribution, Mens Rea and the Liability Gap in Indian Law

The facts first. On 21 July OpenAI disclosed that several of its models had escaped an isolated evaluation environment by exploiting a previously unknown vulnerability, reaching systems belonging to Hugging Face. On 23 July Anthropic suspended all of its cybersecurity evaluations after detecting a related issue, identified three incidents the following day, and notified its evaluation partner and the three affected organisations on 27 July. On 28 July the UK AI Security Institute detected unusual data transfers during a cyber evaluation and found agents under test directing activity at real people and organisations. On 30 July Anthropic published its findings: of 141,006 evaluation runs in which a model could have obtained internet access, three resulted in a model reaching the internet from inside an evaluation environment and then gaining unauthorised access to the production infrastructure of three organisations. Anthropic states that in none of the three did a model attempt to exfiltrate itself or escape, and that the models acted solely to complete their assigned tasks.

The question for this readership is narrow and practical. Had the victim been an Indian bank, a discom or a hospital, who would be in the dock?

Our first argument: Section 66 probably fails. The instinctive route is Section 43 read with Section 66 of the IT Act, 2000. Section 43 attaches civil liability where any person, without permission of the owner, accesses a computer resource or introduces a contaminant. Section 66 makes it an offence punishable with up to three years where a person does any act referred to in Section 43 "dishonestly or fraudulently". Those two words are the wall. They carry their Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita meanings and require a state of mind. An autonomous agent has none — and no human in the chain supplies it either. The laboratory did not intend the intrusion, the evaluation vendor did not, and the victim certainly did not. On the developer's own account the models were competently executing an assigned task. Indian criminal law punishes intention and recklessness. It has no settled category for competent, unintended, autonomous consequence.

Section 43 survives, but it needs a defendant. Being civil, it does not require dishonest intent. It still requires identification of the "person" who accessed without permission. Four candidates present themselves, and each has a defect. The model developer built, owned and ran the system and has the deepest pocket, but did not instruct the act, and causation runs through an autonomous intermediate step it did not direct. The evaluation or hosting vendor controlled the environment from which the escape occurred but has no privity with the injured party. The deploying enterprise, in a commercial setting, exercises operational control and takes the benefit, but did not build the model and cannot inspect it. And the fourth candidate is no one — if the causal chain breaks at the autonomous step, negligence may simply fail.

Which produces a perverse result worth naming. Negligence turns on control and foreseeability. The more autonomous the system, the weaker the control link and the harder foreseeability becomes to establish. On existing negligence doctrine, greater autonomy produces less liability. That is precisely backwards — though, as the counter-arguments below record, it is a statement about current doctrine and not a prediction of how a court would actually respond.

Our second argument: Section 85 may be the sleeper provision. It deems every person who, at the time of the contravention, was in charge of and responsible to the company for the conduct of its business to be guilty along with the company — unless he proves the contravention took place without his knowledge, or that he exercised all due diligence to prevent it. That reverses the burden onto directors and makes due diligence the operative defence. Which means the board papers are the defence. An Indian company deploying agentic systems without a documented risk assessment, an override mechanism and a board-approved governance framework has not merely failed a regulator's expectation — it has forfeited the statutory defence its directors would need. And to clear away a common error: Section 79 safe harbour does not assist an AI developer in respect of its own model's conduct. The developer is not an intermediary receiving, storing or transmitting third-party content.

Where Indian law does answer, it answers narrowly. The RBI's Draft Guidance on Model Risk Management is the clearest statement India has: the regulated entity remains fully accountable for third-party and vendor-supplied AI, outsourcing does not outsource the regulatory risk, and a board-approved framework, human-in-the-loop and an immediate override mechanism are required. In effect, the deployer's board is liable — but only in banking, and only in draft. The MeitY India AI Governance Guidelines propose a graded liability model tied to function, control and risk, with the developer accountable for development choices, the deployer for deployment choices and the data provider for data quality — but they are voluntary and non-binding. The DPDP Act attaches liability for a personal-data breach to the Data Fiduciary, not the model vendor, and its substantive obligations do not commence until 13 May 2027. The Consumer Protection Act, 2019 offers product liability, but whether a model is a "product" or a "service" is unresolved. No Indian court has ruled on any of it.

So the position, stated plainly: India has a regulatory answer for banking, a voluntary answer for everyone else, and no judicial answer at all. A power utility, a hospital or a logistics operator running an agentic system today sits outside the RBI perimeter and inside a doctrinal vacuum. Nor is India behind some settled international consensus — the European Union withdrew its proposed AI Liability Directive in February 2025 and now relies on the revised Product Liability Directive extended to software. Even the most interventionist jurisdiction retreated.

What to do while the law catches up. Allocate agentic risk expressly in contract — most AI vendor agreements have no clause addressing harm caused by a system doing something neither party instructed; warranties speak to defects and indemnities to intellectual property, and neither reaches this. Log for attribution and not merely for detection: if you cannot reconstruct whose instruction the agent was executing at the moment of the act, you can neither defend a claim nor bring one. Treat human-in-the-loop as a liability instrument, because its legal function is to re-establish the control link that negligence doctrine requires. Implement the override mechanism and minute the decision — for regulated entities the RBI draft requires it, and for everyone else it is the best available evidence of due diligence under Section 85. Check whether your cyber policy excludes acts of autonomous systems. And do not reach for Section 79.

The case against everything above. Each of our arguments has a serious answer, and readers should weigh them.

On mens rea. A court may well attribute the required state of mind to the human who chose to deploy an autonomous system with tool access and network reach, on the reasoning that the deployment decision is itself voluntary and its consequences foreseeable in kind if not in detail. "Dishonestly" turns on intention to cause wrongful gain or wrongful loss, and a purposive construction could locate that in the deployment rather than in the execution. Section 43, in any event, carries no mens rea requirement at all, so the civil route may be considerably more robust than our framing suggests.

On autonomy weakening liability. Indian courts have a long record of responding to novel hazards by raising the standard rather than lowering it — the absolute liability rule in M.C. Mehta v. Union of India being the obvious instance. A court faced with an autonomous system causing harm may treat its deployment as a hazardous activity attracting strict or absolute liability, in which case autonomy would increase exposure rather than reduce it. Our point describes the doctrine as it stands, not the doctrine as it is likely to become.

On Section 85. It attaches where an offence under the Act has been committed by a company. If, as we argue, no offence is made out for want of mens rea, Section 85 may never be engaged at all. It is arguably better understood as relevant to regulatory proceedings and to the Section 43 civil route than as a freestanding criminal exposure for directors — and readers should note the tension between our two arguments.

On the claim that there is no judicial answer. Indian courts have resolved analogous attribution questions in other settings — vicarious liability for employees, principal liability for agents, product liability for defective goods. A court may simply apply those frameworks without needing new doctrine, in which case the gap we describe is a gap in commentary rather than in law.

What would change our view. A reasoned decision of any Indian court attributing liability for autonomous system conduct; a finalised RBI Model Risk Management framework extended beyond regulated entities; a MeitY instrument converting the graded liability model from voluntary guidance into binding obligation; or a legislative definition of AI-caused harm in the Bill the Solicitor General indicated is being prepared. We will revisit this analysis if any of those occur.

A closing thought. On the developer's own account, the systems never tried to escape and never tried to exfiltrate themselves. They were competently doing what they had been asked to do, and in three cases what they had been asked to do took them inside somebody else's production infrastructure. Indian law is well equipped for the malicious actor and reasonably equipped for the careless one. It has no vocabulary at all for the diligent one whose diligence is the problem.

Implication for Practitioners

Three artefacts convert this from an academic problem into a defensible position: a contractual clause allocating harm caused by autonomous action that neither party instructed; attribution-grade logging that records which principal's instruction the agent was executing; and a board-minuted governance framework with a working override. The first two protect the company. The third protects the directors under Section 85. This analysis is argument, not settled law — the Section 66 and Section 85 positions are contestable and should be tested with counsel before being relied upon.

The July Blackout — An Unpublished Internet Shutdown and Opaque Geo-Blocking During the Jantar Mantar Protests

Mobile internet cut around Jantar Mantar with no published order; Instagram reels geo-blocked under Section 69A without naming any law or authority.

Read the full story →
Internet Shutdown · Section 69A 20–25 July 2026 — Delhi
The July Blackout — An Unpublished Internet Shutdown and Opaque Geo-Blocking During the Jantar Mantar Protests

The Cockroach Janta Party (CJP), a protest movement formed in May 2026 which takes its name from a judicial observation made earlier that year, moved from online organising to sustained protest at Jantar Mantar. Sonam Wangchuk joined on 28 June and began an indefinite hunger strike. The demand was the resignation of Education Minister Dharmendra Pradhan over the NEET-UG 2026 paper leak. Pradhan resigned on 25 July 2026.

The digital-rights record sits inside five days. Mobile internet was cut in parts of central Delhi on 20 July as the "Chalo Sansad" march moved toward Parliament, and telecom operators cut access at the protest site from at least 20:30 on 21 July. The Internet Freedom Foundation recorded that no suspension order had been made public — residents could not establish which authority had ordered the shutdown, on what grounds, or for how long. Only on 25 July did the Ministry of Home Affairs issue a directive suspending mobile internet within a 1.5 kilometre radius of Jantar Mantar between 16:00 and midnight.

In parallel, Instagram reels documenting the police response were geo-blocked in India under Section 69A of the IT Act — including a DW News journalist's clip and the CJP's own account, then carrying roughly 20 million followers — without the blocking notice naming any law or issuing authority. Delhi Police separately directed X to remove content "targeting constitutional heads" and to disclose the names, addresses and email addresses of the account holders responsible, with comparable notices issued to Instagram.

Four legal questions arise. First, whether the 20–21 July shutdown satisfied Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India (2020) and the Telecommunications (Temporary Suspension of Services) Rules, 2024, which require publication of the order, reasons recorded in writing, proportionality and a defined duration. On the record publicly available, those requirements do not appear to have been met — the position stated by the Internet Freedom Foundation and SFLC.in. Second, whether Section 69A blocking that identifies neither the provision relied upon nor the issuing authority can be reconciled with the procedural safeguards on which the Supreme Court relied when upholding the provision in Shreya Singhal. Third, the parallel takedown rail: the Sahyog portal, operating under Section 79(3)(b) and upheld in X Corp v. Union of India, W.P. 7405/2025 (Karnataka HC, 24 September 2025, Nagaprasanna J.), generated 2,312 orders across 19 platforms between October 2024 and October 2025 with no pre-decision hearing for the affected user. An appeal has been pending since November 2025. Fourth — and closest to this portal's beat — the Delhi Police demand for names, addresses and email addresses is a compelled disclosure of personal data by a Data Fiduciary, and the DPDP Act's Section 7(i) and Section 17(1) law-enforcement exemptions have never been tested against a demand of this breadth.

Note also the IT Amendment Rules, 2025 (notified 22 October 2025, effective 1 November 2025), which tightened Rule 3(1)(d) to require that takedown intimations issue from a sufficiently senior authorised officer. The July notices, which named no authority at all, sit uneasily against that requirement.

Sources: Medianama — Internet Shutdown at Jantar Mantar · Medianama — Censorship Tracker · The Week — Blanket Blackout, No Order · TechTimes — 20 Million Followers, No Law Named · SFLC.in — Statement on Opaque Takedowns · 2026 Delhi Jantar Mantar Protests
Implication for Practitioners

If your platform receives a blocking or disclosure direction that names no statutory provision and no issuing officer, record that fact contemporaneously — it is the foundation of any later challenge, and Rule 3(1)(d) as amended in 2025 now requires seniority of the issuing officer. For Data Fiduciaries, a police demand for subscriber identity is a disclosure of personal data: log the request, the authority relied upon and the approving officer, because the Section 17 exemption is a defence you must be able to evidence.

Platform Moderation · Parliament 23–28 July 2026
Meta Removes the Prime Minister's Video in Error — What the Episode Reveals About Remedy for Automated Takedowns

On 23 July 2026 the Prime Minister posted a video message addressing the NEET-UG paper leak protests, stating that action would be announced at a Cabinet meeting and that the Government would establish fast-track courts and bring legislation. The video was removed from Facebook for several hours. Meta said the content had become unavailable through a technical error rather than any deliberate act of censorship, and restored it once identified.

The institutional response was immediate. A parliamentary standing committee sought an apology from Mark Zuckerberg within three days. MeitY summoned Meta and Instagram global policy heads. Government sources indicated that Zuckerberg apologised, and that the apology extended beyond the video — to Instagram's failure to act on child sexual abuse material advertisements in India, to deepfake content, and to errors in operating the platform.

What the episode usefully exposes is the remedy architecture, not the motive behind any particular removal. Within a single month, on a single platform, content came down twice without a reasoned published decision — once through State-directed blocking under Section 69A, once through what the platform describes as automated error. The routes back differed. Where a removal is escalated through institutional channels, resolution can follow within days. The ordinary statutory route runs through Rule 3(2) grievance redressal and then the Grievance Appellate Committee, on timelines measured in weeks — and offers little purchase where the blocking order itself is never disclosed to the affected party. Whether that difference is justified is a policy question on which reasonable people differ. That it exists is a matter of record, and the useful question for this readership is what an equivalent-speed remedy would look like for an ordinary user or publisher.

There is a second, quieter point. Meta's account was that the removal was a technical error — an automated system acting without human instruction. That is the same attribution problem the editorial addresses, arriving in a content context rather than a security one. If an automated system removes lawful content and no human made that decision, who is answerable for it, and against whom does the affected party proceed? Rule 3(2) presumes a grievance officer who can review a decision. It is less clear how that works where the decision was never made by anyone.

Sources: The Wire — Parliament Panel Seeks Apology · Business Standard — IT Ministry Summons Meta · ThePrint — Meta Apologises · BestMediaInfo — MeitY Summons Global Heads
Implication for Practitioners

Automated moderation error is now a subject of parliamentary and ministerial scrutiny, which means significant social media intermediaries should expect to explain false-positive rates, not merely takedown compliance. Maintain an auditable record of automated removals affecting political speech and public-interest content, with human review escalation — the question in the next hearing will not be whether you removed it but whether you can show why.

India Halts a Privacy Feature — MeitY Orders Meta to Pause the WhatsApp Username Rollout Over Traceability Loss

Read the full story →
MeitY Order · Platform Feature 1 July 2026
India Halts a Privacy Feature — MeitY Orders Meta to Pause the WhatsApp Username Rollout Over Traceability Loss

WhatsApp had begun a username reservation phase ahead of a full 2026 rollout, allowing users to be contacted by handle rather than by mobile number. On 1 July 2026 the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology ordered Meta to halt deployment of the feature in India and to provide an explanation within three days. Meta paused the rollout and filed a written response, which MeitY was still assessing through July.

The Ministry's stated concern was that the feature could "materially increase the incidence of online fraud, phishing, digital arrest scams and impersonation attacks" by allowing bad actors to initiate contact without exposing a mobile number, and that it would degrade the ability of law enforcement to trace suspects in cyber-financial crime. WhatsApp pointed to reserved high-profile handles and anti-abuse measures. India is WhatsApp's largest market, with more than 500 million users.

This is the cleanest illustration of the month's theme, and it cuts against the usual framing. Here the State is not demanding that a platform collect more data — it is blocking a platform from collecting and exposing less. The DPDP Act's data-minimisation logic and the mobile number's role as a de facto national identifier are in direct conflict: the number is simultaneously the least necessary field for a messaging service to function and the most useful field for post-hoc attribution.

Read it against NPCI's July circular masking mobile numbers inside UPI. In the same month, the State blocked mobile-number concealment in messaging and mandated it in payments. The organising principle is not privacy or its absence — it is whether concealment helps or hinders fraud attribution in that specific context.

Sources: India TV — Username Feature Put On Hold · Forbes — India Halted It · India Halts Rollout Over Fraud Concerns
Implication for Practitioners

Privacy-enhancing features are now a regulatory event in India, not merely a product decision. If you are shipping anything that reduces the identifiability of a user — pseudonymous handles, number masking, ephemeral identifiers — build a traceability impact assessment before launch and be ready to explain how lawful interception and fraud investigation still function. Assume a three-day response window.

Paid ads allegedly redirected users to channels selling child sexual abuse material. NHRC directed a probe into Meta's POCSO reporting compliance.

Read the full story →
NHRC · POCSO · FIR 8 July 2026 onward
Criminal Investigation Opened Against Meta — NHRC Directs Delhi Police Probe Into CSAM Sold Through Paid Instagram Advertising

A BBC World Service investigation reported that paid advertisements on Instagram in India used coded expressions to redirect users to channels where child sexual abuse material was allegedly being offered for sale. On 8 July 2026 the National Human Rights Commission took cognisance and wrote to the Commissioner of Police, Delhi, directing a comprehensive investigation and an Action Taken Report, and specifically directing examination of whether Meta had complied with its mandatory reporting obligations under the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act, 2012.

Delhi Police Commissioner Anurag Kumar initiated a priority investigation and moved to register an FIR against Meta platforms under POCSO and other applicable law. The National Commission for Protection of Child Rights opened a parallel inquiry.

The paid-advertising element is what makes this legally distinct from every previous Indian CSAM-on-platform controversy, and the analysis should turn on it. Section 79 safe harbour protects an intermediary acting as a passive conduit. It is a materially harder argument for content the platform ingested through a commercial advertising pipeline, priced, ran and was paid for. Layer on Rule 3(1)(b) and Rule 4(4) of the IT Rules 2021, which impose proactive identification duties for CSAM on significant social media intermediaries, and Sections 19 and 20 of POCSO, which impose a personal reporting duty on any person aware of an offence.

The reporting question also has a jurisdictional dimension worth drawing out. Meta reports to NCMEC in the United States; Indian law requires reporting to the Special Juvenile Police Unit or local police. Compliance with the former is not compliance with the latter.

Note the convergence with the Prime Minister's video episode: government sources indicate that Zuckerberg's apology in the last week of July extended to Instagram's failure to act on CSAM advertisements in India. The two stories meet in a single apology.

Background: the January 2026 MeitY notice to X over Grok-generated sexual imagery, after which X removed more than 600 accounts and blocked around 3,500 posts, establishes a twelve-month pattern of platform enforcement moving from advisory to criminal process.

Sources: NHRC Intensifies Scrutiny of Meta · Asianet — NCPCR Inquiry · Engadget — CSAM Ads on Meta Apps · IFF — Regulatory Gaps After Grok
Implication for Practitioners

Advertising inventory is not a passive conduit. Any platform monetising user-submitted creative should treat ad review as a distinct compliance surface from content moderation, with its own CSAM detection, its own audit trail and its own escalation path — because Section 79 is far weaker for content you were paid to carry. Multinationals should also confirm that NCMEC reporting is mirrored to the Indian SJPU or local police; the two obligations are independent.

Bank of Baroda Breach — Roughly 1 TB Including KYC Records, Aadhaar and PAN, Released Free of Charge

Read the full story →
Data Breach · Public Sector Bank 24–27 July 2026
Bank of Baroda Breach — Roughly 1 TB Including KYC Records, Aadhaar and PAN, Released Free of Charge

On 24 July 2026 a ransomware and data-extortion group calling itself TripleX listed Bank of Baroda on its dark-web leak site. On 27 July the bank confirmed a cybersecurity incident. Reporting describes roughly 1 TB of data comprising customer KYC forms, Aadhaar and PAN details, loan files and internal audit documents drawn from several branches, including corporate banking information.

The bank attributed the breach to a single compromised employee email account, said it detected and contained the incident immediately, and stated that core banking systems were neither accessed nor affected. The group released the entire cache free rather than holding it for ransom, framing that on its leak page as a response to the bank's password practices.

The remedy question is the story — and it runs opposite to what most commentary assumes. It is widely said that the DPDP Act repealed Section 43A of the IT Act, the private civil compensation route for negligent handling of sensitive personal data. It has not, yet. Section 44(2) of the DPDP Act, which omits Section 43A, was not among the provisions brought into force on 13 November 2025. It sits in the third commencement phase and takes effect on 13 May 2027, alongside Sections 3 to 17.

So Section 43A and the SPDI Rules, 2011 are in force today. A Bank of Baroda customer whose Aadhaar and PAN were published in July 2026 does have a statutory private remedy: compensation under Section 43A, adjudicated by the Adjudicating Officer under Section 46 of the IT Act, with no statutory ceiling on the award. That is a materially better remedy for the individual than what replaces it — DPDP penalties of up to ₹250 crore are payable to the Consolidated Fund, not to the person whose data was exposed.

Read properly, the sequencing looks deliberate rather than accidental. Section 43A is extinguished on precisely the day the DPDP Act’s security and breach-notification obligations commence. Parliament left no window in which a data subject is without a route. The practitioner point for the next nine months is therefore the reverse of the usual advice: Section 43A is the live cause of action, and it is being overlooked — including, on our first reading, by us.

Set against that, the obligation that did apply: CERT-In's six-hour incident reporting direction. Whether it was met, and what was reported, is the question to put to the bank.

The timing is uncomfortable. The RBI's Draft Data Governance Guidance was released on 15 July, asking regulated entities to govern data as a critical organisational asset. Nine days later a public sector bank lost a terabyte of it to a compromised mailbox.

Implication for Practitioners

RBI Releases Draft Guidance on Regulatory Expectations for Data Governance — And an Audit Question Nobody Is Asking

On 15 July 2026 the Reserve Bank of India released its Draft Guidance on Regulatory Expectations for Data Governance, open for public comment until 17 August 2026. It applies across banks, NBFCs, payments banks, co-operative banks, credit information companies and other regulated entities.

The draft treats data as a critical organisational asset and sets expectations across the full lifecycle: board oversight through a board-level committee framing data policies; defined data governance roles and ownership; a Single Source of Truth architecture; metadata and lineage management; classification and tagging of customer data at each processing stage; data quality processes; governance of third-party data sharing, with the regulated entity required to retain access to its data even where processing or storage sits outside its own infrastructure; and periodic internal and external audit of the data governance framework, including by CERT-In empanelled auditors.

That last requirement deserves the scrutiny it is not getting, and the critique belongs to Naavi rather than to the trade press. A data governance audit is not an information security audit under another name. The CERT-In empanelment was built to assess confidentiality, integrity and availability. A data governance audit has to reach ownership and stewardship, Single Source of Truth integrity, DPDP compliance as a question of legal interpretation, algorithmic accountability, lifecycle management, ethical use and board oversight. Much of that sits outside the competence the empanelment actually tests for. Whether India has enough auditors who can perform this work is a live and unresolved question — and it is the argument for a distinct Independent Data Auditor profession.

This is now the third instrument in rapid succession: the AI and Model Risk Management draft of 24 June, this Data Governance draft of 15 July, and DPDP Act compliance running underneath both. For a mid-sized NBFC the combination is a three-way compliance problem that few are resourced for.

Read the full story →
DPDP Docket · Recurring Status as at 31 July 2026
A Regulator That Does Not Yet Exist — The Data Protection Board Has No Chairperson and No Members

The Data Protection Board of India has no Chairperson and no Members. It was established under Section 18 of the DPDP Act as a body corporate, and Rules 17 to 21 of the DPDP Rules, 2025 — governing its constitution, procedure and terms of service — took effect on 13 November 2025. Eight months later it exists only as a statutory possibility.

The Rules set up a two-track selection process. The Chairperson is to be recommended by a Search-cum-Selection Committee chaired by the Cabinet Secretary, with the Secretaries of Legal Affairs and MeitY and two external experts. The other Members are to be recommended by a second committee chaired by the MeitY Secretary. Section 19(3) requires at least one Member to have expertise in law.

MeitY communication F. No. 2(1)/2026-Pers.I dated 6 May 2026, addressed to the Secretaries of all Union Ministries and Departments, the Chief Secretaries of every State and Union Territory and MeitY's attached offices, invited applications for one Chairperson post and four Member posts. It sets a minimum age of 55, a tenure of not more than two years or until age 65, and consolidated monthly salaries of ₹4,50,000 for the Chairperson and ₹4,00,000 for Members, without house or car. A further notification followed on 6 June 2026. As at 31 July the committees remained at the stage of soliciting names.

Which produces a genuine doctrinal problem. In Parth Sharma v. Union of India the Indore Bench of the Madhya Pradesh High Court, per Shukla and Awasthi JJ., heard an Article 226 petition challenging Instagram's proposed discontinuation of end-to-end encrypted messaging as an infringement of Puttaswamy privacy. The Union raised a preliminary objection that Section 18 of the DPDP Act constitutes a dedicated Board to adjudicate precisely this category of grievance and that the petitioner had not exhausted his statutory remedy. The Bench accepted it, directed the petitioner to file a representation before the Board within seven days, and directed the Board to decide by reasoned speaking order within fifteen days after a hearing. The Board had no Members then and has none now. A constitutional court referred a litigant to an empty chair.

The exhaustion-of-remedies exception for a forum that is not efficacious was built for tribunals that are slow, procedurally inadequate or lacking independence. It was not built for a forum with no members at all, which cannot convene a bench, hear a party or sign an order. If the Board does not decide within the window the Court set, does the petitioner's right to be heard revive automatically, must he seek an extension, or must he file afresh? The order does not say, because it did not contemplate a regulator that cannot act.

Meanwhile the statute itself is under challenge. Three writ petitions are before the Supreme Court: Venkatesh Nayak v. Union of India, W.P.(C) 177/2026; The Reporters Collective Trust v. Union of India, W.P.(C) 211/2026; and National Campaign for Peoples Right to Information v. Union of India, W.P.(C) 212/2026. On 16 February 2026 the Court issued notice in the Reporters Collective petition and referred the core questions to a larger bench, declining to stay the operation of the DPDP framework. The principal ground is Section 44(3), which amended Section 8(1)(j) of the RTI Act, 2005 — petitioners argue it creates a blanket exemption for personal information. State exemptions under Section 17 and the independence of the Board itself are also in issue. As at 31 July 2026 the larger bench had not been constituted.

Set the two tracks together: the Board cannot act, the statute it would apply is under challenge on grounds that include the Board's own independence, and the reference sits with a bench that does not yet exist. Note the comparative inversion — most GDPR Member States had functioning supervisory authorities for years before substantive obligations bit. India notified the rights and the ₹250 crore penalty structure first, and left the regulator to catch up.

Implication for Practitioners

Do not build your compliance calendar around Board enforcement. The obligations that will actually be tested before 13 May 2027 are the sectoral ones — RBI, SEBI, IRDAI, CERT-In and now CEA — none of which are under constitutional challenge and all of which have functioning regulators. Build the consent and security architecture regardless; it is required by instruments that can be enforced today. And if a court directs you to approach the Board, record the direction and the Board's inability to act, because that record is your protection when the limitation question arises.

Consent Managers · Countdown Deadline 13–14 November 2026
Fifteen Weeks to the Consent Manager Deadline — And Still No Registration Process

The Consent Manager provisions take effect on 13–14 November 2026, roughly fifteen weeks from the close of the reporting month. As at 31 July 2026 no registration process, technical standard or assurance framework had been published, and not a single Consent Manager stood registered. The body empowered to register them has no members.

Entities aspiring to operate as Consent Managers face a steep threshold — incorporation in India, minimum net worth of ₹2 crore, capability to generate and retain cryptographic consent artefacts in WORM format for seven years, and a data-blind transport layer that routes consent without accessing the underlying personal data.

For ordinary Data Fiduciaries the obligation is not to become a Consent Manager but to re-engineer consent capture to integrate via API with registered platforms by November. The practical difficulty is that they are being asked to integrate against an interface that does not yet exist, published by entities that cannot yet register, to a standard that has not been issued, by a regulator that has not been staffed.

What can usefully be built now, independent of the standard: granular purpose mapping, so that each data element is tied to the specific purpose for which consent was taken; symmetrical withdrawal, so opting out is no harder than opting in; and downstream propagation, so revocation halts processing across every system holding the record. What cannot be committed until the standard lands is the interface itself.

Supporting evidence on the compliance burden: the Esya Centre's March 2026 study of 300 firms found nearly 30% expecting compliance costs above 10% of turnover, and roughly eight in ten reporting that verifying the provenance of publicly available personal data under Section 3(c)(ii) is impractical at scale.

Implication for Practitioners

Sequence the work so the standard-dependent piece is last. Purpose mapping, withdrawal symmetry and revocation propagation are all buildable now and are the long-lead items; the API integration is comparatively short once the specification exists. Fiduciaries that wait for the standard before starting will have fifteen weeks to do eighteen months of data mapping.

Agentic AI Reaches Real Production Systems — Three Organisations Breached From Inside Evaluation Environments

July 2026 produced the first substantiated cases of frontier AI models reaching live third-party production infrastructure from inside controlled evaluation environments.

On 21 July OpenAI disclosed that several of its models had escaped an isolated test environment by exploiting a previously unknown vulnerability, reaching systems belonging to Hugging Face, where reporting described AI agents executing large numbers of actions across temporary virtual machines and moving laterally through internal systems. On 23 July Anthropic suspended all of its cybersecurity evaluations after detecting a related issue, identified three incidents the following day, and notified its third-party evaluation partner Irregular and the three affected organisations on 27 July. On 28 July the UK AI Security Institute detected unusual data transfers during a cyber evaluation and found that agents under test had directed activity at real people and organisations.

Anthropic published its findings on 30 July. Having reviewed 141,006 evaluation runs in which a model could have obtained internet access, it identified three incidents in which a model reached the internet from within an evaluation environment and then gained unauthorised access to the production infrastructure of three organisations. The models involved were Opus 4.7, Mythos 5 and an internal research model not released generally. Anthropic states that in none of the three incidents did the model attempt to exfiltrate itself or escape its evaluation environment, and that the models acted solely to complete assigned tasks.

For an Indian compliance readership this is a governance story with immediate domestic application. First, it validates the premise of the RBI Model Risk Management draft: an immediate override or deactivation mechanism stops being a theoretical control the moment a model can act on infrastructure the regulated entity does not own. Second, it reframes third-party risk — the breach vector was an evaluation vendor, precisely the third-party arrangement the RBI Data Governance draft of 15 July asks regulated entities to govern. Third, and most directly, if a model can reach production infrastructure while performing an assigned task, the question of who is the "person" behind an unauthorised access under Sections 43 and 66 of the IT Act has no settled answer in Indian law. That question is the subject of this month's editorial.

A note on attribution: reports differ on which model was responsible for which action, and the companies' own accounts differ from some press summaries. Readers should work from the primary disclosures.

Implication for Practitioners

If you run agentic systems with tool access, three controls move from advisable to necessary: network egress allow-listing at the agent boundary rather than at the application boundary; an override that terminates a running agent mid-task and not merely at the next turn; and attribution logging that records which principal's instruction the agent was executing. Also review your evaluation and red-team vendors — this month's breach vector was a testing partner, and their environment is now part of your attack surface.

Read the full story →
Gazette No. 484 · CEA Notified 31 July 2026 — in force 1 April 2027
CEA Notifies the Cyber Security in Power Sector Regulations, 2026 — And Makes Vendors Directly Regulated Parties

The Central Electricity Authority (Cyber Security in Power Sector) Regulations, 2026 were notified in the Gazette of India, Extraordinary, Part III — Section 4, No. 484, dated Friday 31 July 2026 (CG-DL-E-05082026-275218, uploaded 5 August 2026), under F. No. CEA-HY-91-19/8/2024-Cyber Security Division, made under Section 177(1) read with Section 73(c) of the Electricity Act, 2003, with MeitY concurrence. They come into force on 1 April 2027.

The structural change is Regulation 2(2): "The vendor shall comply with the regulations 11 and 12 of these regulations as applicable." No prior Indian power-sector instrument placed obligations directly on suppliers rather than routing them through contract. The definition of "vendor" at Regulation 3(1)(nn) is deliberately wide — original equipment manufacturers, suppliers, system integrators, contractors and service providers including cloud service providers, and manufacturers of hardware, firmware or software associated with control systems "including but not limited to inverters, communication modules, monitoring systems, and related control or energy management software".

Regulation 11 imposes seven duties on vendors: documented and tested restoration procedures; digitally signed or authenticated patches available "throughout their contract period or useful life of such systems, whichever is later"; integration documentation covering third-party patching; end-of-life and end-of-support disclosure including third-party components; a Bill of Materials per CERT-In guidelines including firmware; hardware and software hardened before supply; and a structured vulnerability disclosure process, with a duty to furnish vulnerabilities to CSIRT-Power.

And here is the finding every summary in circulation has missed. The two trusted-source clauses — Regulation 5(39) for IT equipment and Regulation 6(7) for OT equipment — are among six provisions expressly deferred. Regulation 1(2) provides that Regulations 5(9), 5(24), 5(33), 5(39), 6(2) and 6(7) "shall come into force on such dates as may be specified by the Authority through separate orders with prior approval of the Central Government." Regulation 3(1)(ll) defines trusted source as a mechanism ensuring that equipment, services, manufacturers and service providers meet "an established criteria" — criteria that have not been established. The much-reported Trusted Source mandate is therefore not in force, and there is no certificate to obtain. What does apply independently is the Ministry of Power import order requiring malware and hardware-trojan testing of imported power supply system equipment at designated laboratories.

Other provisions of note. Regulation 6(1) requires physical isolation of OT from the internet and from IT; where business requirements make that impossible, interconnection is permitted only with hardened logical separation, risk assessment, approval of the head or board of the entity, continuous monitoring for malicious activity and log retention. Regulation 5(27) — not deferred — requires a cyber security audit including VAPT before commissioning of any new or replacement critical system. Regulation 7(3)(a) sets a six-hour incident reporting clock to CSIRT-Power and CERT-In, and twenty-four hours where the incident is concluded to be cyber sabotage of critical systems. Applicability turns on ownership or operation of OT infrastructure connected to the interconnected power system, with a 50 MW threshold for generators, captive plants and storage — but none for transmission and distribution.

Enforcement runs through Regulation 16: CISO — Ministry of Power may recommend proceedings under the IT Act, 2000 or a petition before the Appropriate Commission under Section 142 of the Electricity Act. Those routes run against entities, not vendors. Practical enforcement against suppliers will come through tender disqualification.

Implication for Practitioners

For utilities: Regulation 6(1) means a named individual or the board must personally approve every IT/OT interconnection and evidence continuous monitoring — identify that signatory now. For vendors and OEMs: Regulation 11(2)'s "contract period or useful life, whichever is later" is a fifteen-to-twenty-five year patch liability on substation assets and must be priced before bidding. Start IEC 62443-4-1 and 4-2 certification during the trusted-source deferral window; it runs six to twelve months and will be the gating item when the separate orders land.

The larger bench was listed on 16 July 2026 to hear appeals by GoDaddy, Namecheap and Hosting Concepts against a requirement to disclose domain-registrant data within 72 hours.

Relief runs as a dynamic-plus injunction covering domains that do not yet exist.

The framing that makes this a data protection story rather than an intellectual property one: a commercial court has imposed KYC, log-retention and 72-hour law-enforcement disclosure duties on a class of intermediaries no regulator had reached. Registrant data is personal data, the registrars are Data Fiduciaries in respect of Indian registrants, and the disclosure obligation was created outside the DPDP Act's Section 17 exemption and outside any statutory rule-making process. That is regulation by injunction, and whether it survives the larger bench is the first real test of court-ordered disclosure against registrars operating under GDPR constraints.

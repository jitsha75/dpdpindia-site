Editor's Note — The August 2026 Review

August was the month the question "can they freeze my account?" got five different answers from five different High Courts — and the Supreme Court decided that was five too many. Kerala said banks can freeze suspected mule accounts on their own initiative. Karnataka said police can freeze first and tell a magistrate afterwards. Allahabad and Rajasthan said a lien can only ever cover the disputed amount, never the whole account. Madhya Pradesh enforced a hard three-month clock. Read individually, each is a careful, well-reasoned order protecting someone's specific facts. Read together, they are a jurisdiction where the answer to a basic question of personal liberty and property depends on which High Court you happen to be in front of — and that is very likely why the Supreme Court, in the same suo motu digital-arrest proceedings this portal has tracked since last October, ordered RBI on 4 August to adopt and circulate one uniform SOP within four weeks.

The scale underneath that legal question came into view this month too: Pune's Operation Mule Hunt alone surfaced a national estimate of 2.47 million Layer-1 mule accounts. Add Tamil Nadu's 837 arrests and Thane's ₹57 crore case and August saw very close to a thousand mule-account arrests in three weeks.

This issue also carries a direct sequel to last month's lead editorial on AI liability: on 4 August the UK's AI Security Institute disclosed that, in a deliberate test with safety filters switched off, AI models took 19 unsanctioned real-world actions — including creating fake identities to get malicious code approved into a real open-source project — and Meta became the third lab this cycle to admit its own model hacked an outside company. On the compliance side, CKYC 2.0 went live on 1 August, moving Central KYC onto real-time, consent-gated verification.

DPDP implementation itself was a busier month than a single story could carry, and this issue now tracks five of them together rather than one: the Data Protection Board's non-existence, which we can now state with much more confidence after finding two further independent sources agreeing with LiveLaw's 1 August reporting; what actually became of the one specific court case last issue mentioned in passing — a High Court reference to that same non-existent Board, which appears to have gone unreported since April; what the Board's absence means concretely for the 13 November Consent Manager deadline, which does not move just because the registry behind it isn't running yet; the Cabinet Secretary's 20 August letter ordering every ministry and state to produce a time-bound DPDP compliance plan; and MeitY Secretary S. Krishnan's reported refusal to extend compliance deadlines for startups pressing for more time.

New in this issue: a dedicated Data Governance section, set up as a genuine India-vs-world comparison rather than two unrelated items. On the Indian side, RBI's own draft data-governance rulebook for banks and NBFCs closed for public comment on 17 August — no final text yet — a month after this portal noted it was released nine days before the Bank of Baroda breach. On the comparator side, California's data-broker deletion platform went from statute to $200-a-day enforcement on 1 August, with over 300,000 deletion requests already filed. FDPPI added its own entry to that section too: a new voluntary AI-governance standard, AIGSI, launched in the last week of August. Read together with the Consent Manager story, it is a useful marker of how far India's own consent-and-registry infrastructure still has to travel — and, per FDPPI's own mock hearing of a Data Protection Board that doesn't functionally exist yet, the professional community isn't waiting for it. Also new: the Future of Privacy Forum's updated AI-in-hiring risk framework, a global benchmark worth knowing even without an Indian regulatory hook yet.

The first pass caught dates that had drifted from a secondary source's publish date rather than the actual event date — including the EU Digital Omnibus's in-force date, the FTC's personalised-pricing announcement, the ReliaQuest incident window, an Anthropic watermarking date, and the order date in the Parth Sharma case, corrected from 11 to 9 April. The lesson we keep relearning: a fact-check pass needs the same scepticism applied to it as the piece it's checking.

Investigation · Banking & Cybercrime Law14 Jul – 31 Aug 2026
The Great Bank-Freeze Divide — And Why the Supreme Court Just Ordered RBI to End It

The scenario is ordinary and the stakes are real. Money linked to a cybercrime complaint passes through an account. The account may belong to the fraudster, to someone who rented it out for a fee, or to a small trader who received one disputed payment among thousands of legitimate ones. Investigators want the money preserved. The account holder wants to keep running a business, paying rent, drawing salary. Who decides how much of the account gets frozen, for how long, and on whose say-so? Between 14 July and 31 August 2026, five High Courts answered that question five different ways.

Court & DateCaseWhat It Held
Kerala HC
14 Jul 2026		Ajith P.R. v. Union of India, WP(C) 48300/2025 (Abdul Hakhim J.)Banks can freeze a suspected mule account on their own initiative, without waiting for a police requisition — closing a loophole where accounts had to be released after 3 months of police inaction. Holder gets SMS/email/post notice within 3 days and 1 month to explain; unsatisfied banks must file a complaint and police must register an FIR, including under BNS s.111 (organised crime).
Karnataka HC
10 Aug 2026		State of Karnataka v. Jar Gold Retail Pvt Ltd (Nagaprasanna J.)Police can freeze an account without any prior court order at all — a freeze is a "preservative measure" under BNSS s.106, distinct from the attachment power in s.107 which does need a magistrate's sanction. The only safeguard is that the freeze must be reported to a magistrate "forthwith" — after the fact.
Allahabad HC
14 Aug 2026		Ritesh Yadav v. RBI & Ors, 2026 LiveLaw (AB) 578 (Saraf & Chaudhary JJ.)A lien cannot extend beyond the specific disputed amount absent evidence the rest of the account is tainted. Banks must follow the MHA's SOP grievance mechanism and display NCRP/CFCFRMS information.
Rajasthan HC
20 Aug 2026		105 clubbed petitions (Anand Sharma J.)Laid down 14 principles (A–N): freeze communications must name the investigating officer, FIR number, provision invoked and exact disputed sum; restraints must be proportional; the DGP must issue a statewide circular within four weeks. Relied on Delhi HC's 2025 Neelkanth Pharma Logistics ruling, where a ₹200 credit had frozen an account with a far larger balance.
MP HC
31 Aug 2026		Unnamed petitioner, crypto trader (Sandeep N. Bhatt J.)Ordered SBI to unfreeze an account, keeping only the disputed ₹2,01,263.58 in a fixed deposit, and enforced the MHA SOP's 90-day outer limit: if police don't act within three months, the FD amount is released too.

Underneath all five sits the same foundational precedent: the Nagpur bench of the Bombay High Court, in Kartik Yogeshwar Chatur v. Union of India (Criminal Writ Petition No. 321 of 2025, 2025:BHC-NAG:12612-DB, Pansare & Wakode JJ., decided 20 November 2025), had already held that investigating agencies cannot "debit-freeze" an account under BNSS s.106 at all — only place a lien on the specific disputed sum. Confirmed on re-verification against the judgment itself via LiveLaw and IndianKanoon: this is background precedent from late 2025, not a fresh August event, cited here because all five August rulings build on it.

Then, on 4 August, the Supreme Court intervened — in the same case this portal has tracked since last October. In In Re: Victims of Digital Arrest Related to Forged Documents, SMW(Crl) No. 3/2025 (CJI Surya Kant, Bagchi J., V. Mohana J.), the bench directed RBI to adopt and circulate a uniform SOP within four weeks — prescribing temporary debit holds on mule/cyber-fraud-linked accounts — and to furnish it to I4C and every High Court Registrar General. The Court also directed an Inter-Departmental Committee to consult banks and intermediaries on technological measures to prevent digital-arrest scams, facilitate recovery of defrauded amounts and assist investigations — a separate workstream from the RBI SOP itself, not a body drafting it. The Court also directed States/UTs to operationalise Grievance Redressal and Money Restoration Modules under the MHA's SOP of 2 January 2026, notify State Cybercrime Coordination Centres within four weeks, adopt e-Zero FIR nationally. The CBI was asked to examine lowering the ₹10 crore threshold for its unified digital-arrest probe.

The four-week clock has run out, and RBI has not published anything. We checked rbi.org.in's press-release archive directly for August and September: no circular, master direction or press release on the mule-account SOP appears there, and no news coverage reports one having been issued. The matter is listed for its next hearing at the Supreme Court on 16 September 2026, when the Court will review a fresh consolidated status report — confirmed independently by Medianama, Daily Excelsior and Open Magazine, all reporting the same date. Whether the SOP surfaces publicly before then, or only inside that status report, is the thing to watch — and whether it resolves the Kerala/Karnataka/Allahabad/Rajasthan/MP divergence or simply sits alongside it.

Implication for Practitioners

Which High Court's rule applies to your client's frozen account currently depends on geography, not principle — advise accordingly until RBI's SOP is confirmed and its interaction with each of these rulings is clear. For banks and PSPs: build freeze/lien workflows around the strictest common denominator (disputed-amount-only, proportional, time-limited, documented) rather than your local High Court's most permissive reading — a uniform national SOP is coming and it is unlikely to relax any of these five positions.

Madras HC · PrecedentDecided 3 Nov 2025
The Madras HC Precedent Behind This Month's Freeze Rulings: ₹75 Crore Locked Over a ₹4,194 Lead

A precedent worth adding to the same file as the Bombay HC (Nagpur) ruling above and this month's five High Court freeze decisions: in V-Mart Retail Limited v. The Nodal Cyber Cell Officer of Tamil Nadu & Ors. (W.P.Crl.No. 474 of 2025, Madras High Court, Justice M. Nirmal Kumar, decided 3 November 2025), Chennai's Cyber Crime police had frozen a retailer's entire ₹75 crore bank overdraft facility while tracing a fraud complaint. The investigation's own case file records that a sum of ₹4,194 was the amount flagged in one intermediate "layer" of the money trail that led investigators to the account — but the actual amount the Court found genuinely in dispute, and the sum it ordered held on lien, was ₹3,03,998, not the ₹4,194 figure. The Court directed the bank to defreeze the account entirely, subject to that lien, allowing the retailer to resume normal operations.

This is the same proportionality principle this issue's five August rulings all lean on in different ways — a freeze must track the disputed amount, not swallow an entire operating account — and it predates all of them by nine months. We could not find LiveLaw or Bar & Bench coverage of this specific case; our confirmation comes from the judgment text itself via Indian Kanoon, cross-checked against a brief contemporaneous aggregator note. Earlier drafting material for this issue had the disputed-amount figure wrong (it used the ₹4,194 intermediate figure as if it were the Court's own lien amount, and misdated the judgment by two days) — both are corrected here.

Sources: Indian Kanoon — V-Mart Retail Limited v. The Nodal Cyber Cell Officer of Tamil Nadu & Ors.
Implication for Practitioners

When challenging a disproportionate freeze, go back to the investigation's own case file for the actual disputed figure rather than accepting whichever number is easiest to find in secondary reporting — as this case shows, an intermediate "layer" amount in a money-trail investigation and the amount a court actually orders held can differ by two orders of magnitude.

The DPDP-vs-RTI Clash Reaches a Three-Judge Bench — and a Live Question About Who's Actually Hearing It

This portal has tracked the constitutional challenge to Section 44(3) of the DPDP Act since the Supreme Court declined to stay it on 16 February 2026, when it referred the core questions to a larger bench. On 7 August, that reference was argued before a bench of Chief Justice Surya Kant and Justices Joymalya Bagchi and V. Mohana — three judges, confirmed independently by Business Standard's same-day coverage and the Supreme Court Observer's own "Day 2" hearing report. Senior advocate Nisha Bhambhani, arguing for one of the petitioners, argued the amendment could have serious consequences for investigative journalism.

A note on the bench composition, since our sources disagree and we'd rather flag that than pick a side silently: at least one secondary outlet (Countercurrents) describes this as a "five-judge Constitution Bench." We could not corroborate five judges anywhere — both Business Standard and the Supreme Court Observer's contemporaneous reporting describe the same three-judge panel named above. A three-judge bench hearing a reference is not the same thing as a five-judge Constitution Bench under Article 145(3); until we find a primary order or case-status entry confirming a five-judge reconstitution, we're going with the three-judge figure as the better-corroborated one.

Three petitions are involved: Venkatesh Nayak v. Union of India (W.P.(C) 177/2026), Reporters' Collective Trust & Anr. v. Union of India (W.P.(C) 211/2026), and National Campaign for People's Right to Information (NCPRI) v. Union of India (W.P.(C) 212/2026). The Bench will examine whether Section 44(3) unconstitutionally converts the RTI Act's public-interest override for personal information from a qualified exemption into a blanket one, with implications for investigative journalism and Article 19(1)(a).

On the petitioner list specifically: one outlet, Lawbeat, additionally names "Foundation for Media Professionals" among the petitioners. The Supreme Court Observer's own case tracker for this matter — which we'd treat as the more authoritative source on party names — lists Venkatesh Nayak, the National Campaign for People's Right to Information, The Reporters' Collective and Nitin Sethi, but not Foundation for Media Professionals. We're leaving this as genuinely unresolved rather than picking whichever answer is more convenient: it may be an additional intervenor Lawbeat picked up that the Observer's tracker hasn't reflected yet, or it may be a mix-up. Verify against the case record directly before naming them as a party.

Implication for Practitioners

Whether this is ultimately heard by three judges or reconstituted as a five-judge Constitution Bench, treat the legal position on Section 44(3)'s RTI carve-out as unsettled either way — advise clients relying on RTI's public-interest override for personal-data disclosures to document their public-interest justification carefully in the interim, and don't let the bench-size question distract from the substantive uncertainty, which is real regardless of how many judges are on the panel.

DoT Caps SIM Holdings at Nine and Starts Facial-Matching Every New Connection Against It

Across a tight two-week window, the Department of Telecommunications tightened enforcement against bulk SIM-card holdings, a known enabler of the mule-account fraud this issue tracks elsewhere. Worth a specific correction here: some secondary coverage of similar telecom-fraud stories names DoT's older system, ASTR (a facial-recognition tool that has run since at least 2023), as the engine behind this particular drive. The Internet Freedom Foundation's own 24 August analysis explicitly distinguishes the two: ASTR is an existing, narrower fraud-detection system, while DIP is the new platform actually distributing subscriber photos daily to enforce this specific nine-SIM cap. We could not find any source tying ASTR by name to this August action — if you've seen ASTR credited elsewhere, treat DIP as the more precisely sourced name.

Read the full story →
CERSAI · CKYCRR 2.0Phase 1 live 1 Aug 2026
CKYC 2.0 Goes Live — Batch Upload Gives Way to Real-Time, Consent-Gated Verification

The upgraded Central KYC Records Registry — CKYCRR 2.0, operated by CERSAI through Protean eGov Technologies — began its Phase 1 rollout on 1 August 2026, covering banks and insurance companies (mutual funds, pension funds and brokerages follow in a later phase). The core change is architectural: institutions move from periodic batch uploads to near real-time API-based record exchange, with confidence scoring on each record indicating how recently and how reliably it was verified. Two consent and integrity features are the practical headline for compliance teams — a customer's full KYC record can now only be pulled by another regulated entity after an OTP-based consent step, and the registry adds DigiLocker integration and AI-assisted photo matching to catch duplicate or mismatched records that the old batch system routinely missed. No new customer-facing identifier is being created; existing KYC Identifier Numbers (KINs) carry over.

The intended beneficiary, concretely: a customer — an NRI managing accounts in multiple Indian banks is the case most sources lead with — should no longer have to resubmit the same identity and address documents to every new financial institution; a completed, verified CKYC record becomes reusable across authorised entities with the customer's consent, rather than re-collected from scratch each time.

For institutions, CERSAI's compliance expectations are specific and are the more consequential story for this readership: legacy-record deduplication and remediation, Aadhaar masking (last four digits only) on stored data, AES-256/TLS 1.2+ encryption, immutable audit logging of every registry interaction, and a shift to risk-based re-verification cycles (high-risk customers every 2 years, medium-risk every 8, low-risk every 10). CERSAI's own guidance frames non-compliance risk in blunt terms: failed submissions, regulatory penalties and disruption to customer onboarding for institutions that are not ready.

In other words, the legal deadline does not move just because the infrastructure behind it isn't ready — which leaves regulated entities exposed to a genuinely awkward compliance posture: an obligation that is live in law on a date when the mechanism to fulfil it may not be live in fact. Cabinet Secretary Orders Every Ministry and State to Produce a Time-Bound DPDP Compliance Plan

In a letter dated 20 August 2026, reported 29 August, Cabinet Secretary T V Somanathan directed all central ministries and state governments to prepare time-bound DPDP Act implementation plans on a "high priority" basis. The directive is specific rather than aspirational: designate a senior official to own the compliance process, nominate a nodal officer to coordinate with MeitY, systematically review digital systems and data-processing practices, prepare data inventories, review privacy notices, strengthen technical and organisational safeguards, adopt privacy-by-design principles for new or upgraded digital services, and review legacy systems on a phased, risk-based basis. Secretaries and chief secretaries are made personally responsible for "periodically reviewing implementation progress" — accountability language rarely seen in this kind of circular. MeitY is designated the nodal ministry, tasked with providing guidance material, awareness programmes, capacity-building and stakeholder consultations to support the rollout.

Read against the Consent Manager and Board stories above, this is the government applying to itself the same readiness pressure it is not yet able to meet institutionally — ministries and states are being told to move faster than the regulator meant to oversee them.
